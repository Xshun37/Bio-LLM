Dimethylcelecoxib, a non-COX-2 inhibiting derivative of celecoxib, inhibits PGE2 synthesis by transcriptional in-
hibition of mPGES-1. Previously we demonstrated that DMC downregulates EGR1 expression and increases nu-
clear NF-κB in human cervical cancer cells (HeLa) [1]. Both transcription factors are important regulators of
mPGES-1 expression. Here we show that treatment of HeLa cells with DMC inhibits EGR1 promoter activity by
inﬂuencing the transactivation activity of NF-κB. Mutation of the NF-κB motif as well as downregulation of
NF-κB(p65)RelA using siRNA repealed the inhibitory effect of DMC on the EGR1 promoter. The transactivation
activity of NF-κB is regulated by various co-activators or co-repressors. One of these co-repressors is HDAC1.
DMC did not inﬂuence HDAC1 expression, but the HDAC activity was enhanced under DMC inﬂuence. After
DMC treatment NF-κB co-immunoprecipitated with HDAC1. Electromobility shift assays depicted an increased
interaction between NF-κB–HDAC1 and DNA containing NF-κB binding motives. Performing CHIP-assays we ﬁ-
nally demonstrated the interaction of NF-κB and HDAC1 at the EGR1 promoter that was in part reversed by the
HDAC1 inhibitor trichostatin A. Using siRNA against HDAC1 we could repeal the inhibitory effect of DMC on the
EGR1 promoter. In conclusion we demonstrated that treatment of HeLa cells with DMC leads to an enhanced for-
mation of a complex consisting of NF-κB and HDAC1 that binds to the EGR1 promoter resulting in downregula-
tion of EGR1 expression which plays a major role for transcriptional inhibition of mGPES-1 expression. How
these effects of DMC may contribute to a potential therapeutical beneﬁt of various diseases is discussed.

1. Introduction
Prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) plays an important role in physiological
and pathophysiological processes. It is produced along the enzy-
matic pathway consisting of phospholipases, cyclooxygenases and
PGE synthases. The mPGES-1, one of three PGE2 synthases, is barely
basally expressed, but is inducible by different stimuli and frequently
co-expressed with COX-2. COX-2, mPGES-1 and PGE2 are enhanced
during pain, inﬂammatory processes and in several cancer cells [2–5].
Inhibition of PGE2 production is therefore a common mechanism to
treat pain and inﬂammatory diseases using unselective or selective
COX-2 inhibitors such as ibuprofen and diclofenac, or celecoxib and
etoricoxib, respectively. Due to the fact that long-term treatment with
COX-inhibitors is associated with severe side effects like thromboem-
bolic events, liver and skin toxicity, alternatives are sought after to in-
hibit PG production, especially PGE2 production more selectively. DMC
(dimethylcelecoxib={4-[5-(2,5-dimethylphenyl)-3(triﬂuoromethyl)-
1H-pyrazol-1yl]benzenesulfonamide}) is a derivative of celecoxib, but
without cyclooxygenase inhibiting properties up to high concentra-
tions. Nevertheless, after stimulation of various human cell lines with
IL-1β/TNFα and simultaneous treatment with DMC PGE2 synthesis
is inhibited [6]. Our investigations revealed that DMC inhibits
mPGES-1 activity and prevents mPGES-1 upregulation after IL-1β/TNFα
treatment in these cells [6]. We demonstrated that DMC inhibits

mPGES-1 expression by interacting with EGR1 and NF-κB [1]. EGR1 is
the key regulation factor of mPGES-1 expression [7]. DMC leads on
the one hand to a repression of EGR1 expression and on the other
hand it induces NF-κB translocations into the nucleus. Further inves-
tigations revealed out that DMC treatment leads to an enhanced bind-
ing capacity of NF-κB to speciﬁc NF-κB binding motives, but this was
accompanied by an inhibition of transcription [1]. The transcriptional
activity of NF-κB is regulated by different mechanisms. In unstimulated
cells NF-κB complexes are retained in the cytoplasm by a family of in-
hibitory proteins known as inhibitors of NF-κB (IκBs) [8]. After activa-
tion of the cells NF-κB is released of this IκBs and translocates to the
nucleus. The NF-κB family consists of ﬁve members (RelA(p65), RelB,
c-Rel, p50/p105, p52/p100), that form complexes from their homo- or
heterodimer. Furthermore, regulation of NF-κB transcription activity is
also mediated by various posttranslational modiﬁcations like phosphor-
ylation, acetylation, methylation and ubiquitination mechanisms at sev-
eral amino-acid residues [9]. It is known that NF-κB can interact with
proteins acting as transcriptional co-activators or co-repressors [8].
However, a NF-κB binding site is located at the anti-sense strand of
the mPGES-1 promoter. Therefore, it is questionable whether NF-κB
has a direct or indirect effect on mPGES-1 transcriptional regulation or
whether another NF-κB binding site is located further upstream of the
mPGES-1 promoter [10]. The DMC mediated downregulation of EGR1
may also be related to transcriptional regulation mechanisms. The
human EGR1 promoter contains several putative transcription factor
binding sites [11] which also includes a NF-κB binding motif, that has
been shown to be involved in transcriptional regulation of EGR1 expres-
sion [12].
To further clarify the molecular mechanism leading to inhibition
of EGR1 transcription and therefore inhibition of mPGES-1 and PGE2
production after DMC treatment we investigated the role of NF-κB
in this pathway.
2. Materials and methods
2.1. Cells and reagents
HeLa (human cervix carcinoma) cells were purchased from
“Deutsche Sammlung für Mikroorganismen und Zellkulturen” (DSMZ,
Braunschweig, Germany) and cultured in RPMI medium 1640. Medium
contained high glucose, GlutaMAX, 10% FCS. Medium and FCS were
purchased from Invitrogen (Germany) as well as 100 units/ml peni-
cillin G and 100 mg/ml streptomycin. Cells were cultured at 37 °C in
an atmosphere containing 5% CO2. DMC was synthesized by WITEGA
Laboratorien Berlin-Adlershof GmbH. The identity and purity of
dimethylcelecoxib was determined using 1H NMR and liquid chro-
matography tandem mass spectrometry (LC/MS–MS) as described
previously [13–14] and was N99%. Recombinant human interleukin
1 beta (IL-1β) and recombinant human tumor necrosis factor alpha
(TNFα) were purchased from PeproTech (London, UK).
2.2. Plasmid constructs
The genomic DNA fragment of the human EGR1 promoter
(Fig. 1A) was ampliﬁed by PCR with primers containing a KPNI or
XhoI restriction site (5′-ggc cgg tac ccg tcg gtt cgc tct cac ggt ccc-3′
and 5′-gca gct cga ggc tgg atc tct cgc gac tcc-3′) using genomic DNA
of HeLa cells as template and HotStartTaq Plus DNA Polymerase
(Qiagen). PCR conditions: 10 min 95 °C, 3 min 95 °C, 1 min 63 °C,
2 min 72 °C, repeated 30 times, ending with a ﬁnal 72 °C step for
10 min. The resulting EGR1 promoter PCR product was digested
with XhoI and KPNI and cloned into the pGL4.10 vector (Promega;
a kind gift of AG Suess) and further designated as EGR1 promoter
(Fig. 1B). The EGR1 promoter construct containing a mutated NF-κB
binding site, freely adapted from Thierry Virolle [12], was generated
by site directed mutagenesis PCR with primers containing the KPNI or
a MluI restriction site (5′-ggc cgg tac ccg tcg gtt cgc tct cac ggt ccc-3′
and 5′-tgc tcc tta tat gga cgc gtc ggg tcg cag ct-3′) using the EGR promot-
er construct of the pGL4.10 vector (Fig. 1B) as template and HotStartTaq
Plus DNA Polymerase (Qiagen). PCR conditions: 10 min 95 °C, 3 min
95 °C, 1 min 68.5 °C, 2 min 72 °C, repeated 30 times, ending with a
ﬁnal 72 °C step for 10 min. The resulting PCR product was used as prim-
er in a second PCR with an additional primer containing a XhoI restric-
tion site (5′-gca gct cga ggc tgg atc tct cgc gac tcc-3′). Template was
again the EGR promoter construct of the pGL4.10 vector (Fig. 1B). The
resulting PCR product was digested with XhoI and KPNI and cloned
into the pGL4.10 vector and further designated as EGR1 promoter mut
NF-κB (Fig. 1B). The MluI restriction site was used as positive control
to verify the insertion of the point mutation. All PCR products were ver-
iﬁed by sequencing.
2.3. Transfection and luciferase assay
Transient transfection was performed with TransPass HeLa trans-
fection reagent (New England Biolabs) according to the manufac-
turer's protocol for adherent cells. HeLa cells (1×105) were seeded
on Greiner 6 well or 12 well plates with 1 ml or 500 μl of culture me-
dium 1 day before transfection. The cells were transfected with 1.5 μg
or 0.7 μg of the distinct ﬁreﬂy luciferase reporter vectors and 1 μg or
0.5 μg of the Renilla luciferase control reporter vector (pRL-TK, Pro-
mega) using 7.5 μl or 4 μl TransPass HeLa transfection reagent. In
siRNA experiments 25 nM Silencer© Select siRNA of NF-κB(p65)
RelA, HDAC1 or Silencer© Select Negative Control purchased from
Ambion (Invitrogen GmbH; Darmstadt; Germany) was used. 1 day
after transfection, the cells were incubated for 24 h with or without
TNFα (5 ng/ml)+IL-1β (1 ng/ml) and simultaneously treated with
different concentrations of DMC (0.1–20 μM) or 0.1% DMSO (dimeth-
yl sulfoxide) (DMC was solved in DMSO). The luciferase assay was
performed with luciferase substrate puffer (Tricine 20 mM; (MgCO3)
4 Mg(OH)2×5H2O 2.67 mM; MgSO4×7H2O 1.07 mM; Ethylenedia
mine-tetraacetic acid (EDTA) 100 μM; 3,3′-Diethylthiatricarbocyanine
iodide (DTTC) 0.33 mg/ml; Co-Enzyme A 0.213 mg/ml; D-Luciferin
470 mM) and a luminometer (Mithras LB 940, Berthold technologies
GmbH & Co.KG, Bad Wildbad, Germany) according to the manufac-
turer's instructions. The activity of ﬁreﬂy luciferase was normalized to
the RLU of Renilla luciferase (0.1 M NaCl; 25 mM Tris–HCl [pH 7.5];
0.147 mg/ml CaCl2; 900 nM Coelenterazine). Data represent the
mean+/−S.E.M. of at least three independent experiments.

2.5. Nuclear extract preparation and Electrophoretic Mobility Shift Assay
(EMSA)
HeLa cells were seeded and treated as described above for western
blot assay. Nuclear extracts for EMSA were prepared as following:
Cells were gently scrapped and washed in ice-cold phosphate-buffered
saline. Then the cells were allowed to swell in ice-cold hypotonic buffer
containing 10 mM HEPES–KOH [pH 7.9]; 10 mM KCl; 1.5 mM MgCl2;
0.5 mM DTT; 1 mM PMSF; 1 mM Na3VO4 and Roche complete for
15 min on ice. Afterwards NP-40 was added to a ﬁnal concentration of
0.5%. The nuclei were recovered by centrifugation (2500 rpm, 5 min,
4 °C). The pellets were resuspended in high-salt buffer containing
20 mM HEPES [pH 7.9]; 420 mM NaCl; 1.2 mM MgCl2; 0.5 mM DTT;
0.2 mM EDTA; 25% glycerol; 0.5 mM PMSF; 1 mM Na3VO4 and Roche
complete, followed by incubation on ice for 20 min. The nuclear extracts
were recovered by centrifugation and protein concentration was deter-
mined by the method of Bradford (Bio-Rad). Synthetic double-stranded
oligonucleotides corresponding to the NF-κB binding motif 5′-
GGGRNNYYCC-3′ (5′-agt tga ggg gac ttt ccc agg-3′) 5′-labeled with
IRD700 were used. For competition assays the identical unlabeled oligo-
nucleotide was used. Binding reactions were conducted with 5 μg of nu-
clear extract, 1 μg of poly-(dI-dC) and 250 fmol IRD700 end labeled
oligonucleotide in binding buffer (12 mM HEPES [pH 7.9]; 4 mM Tris–
HCl [pH 7.9]; 60 mM KCl; 0.6 mM DTT; 0.6 mM EDTA; 5 mM MgCl2
and 12% glycerol) at 22 °C for 30 min in a ﬁnal volume of 20 μl. For
supershift assays, an antibody against NF-κB(p65)RelA or HDAC1
(3 μg/reaction) was incubated with the reaction mixture for 30 min at
22 °C before the addition of IRD700 labeled oligonucleotide. For com-
petition assays, 100-fold molar excess consensus oligonucleotide
was used. Binding complexes were resolved in a non-denaturating
4% polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis in a tris borate buffer
system, after which the gel was analyzed with an infrared Odyssey
scanner.
2.6. Immunoprecipitation
Whole cell extract was produced as described for western blot.
Whole cell extract was precleared by incubation with 1.0 μg of the
appropriate control IgG (corresponding to the host species of the pri-
mary antibody (anti-rabbit, Santa Cruz)), together with 20 μl of ap-
propriate suspended Protein A/G-Agarose (Santa Cruz) for 30 min
at 4 °C. Bead pellet was kept as negative control (NC). 2 μg of
HDAC1 antibody (rabbit polyclonal, HDAC1, Cell Signaling) was
added to 500 μg total cellular protein and incubated overnight at
4 °C. 30 μg of cell lysates was used as positive control (PC). 20 μl of
Protein A/G PLUS-Agarose was added and incubated at 4 °C on a ro-
tating device for 1 h. Precipitates were collected by centrifugation
at 2500 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C. Pellet and NC were washed three
times (10 min) with 1.0 ml IP buffer. Last washing was performed
with Tris–HCl [pH 7.4] for 10 min. After ﬁnal wash, pellets were
resuspended in Lämmlibuffer and analyzed by SDS PAGE.

3. Results
3.1. DMC inhibits EGR1 promoter activity by inﬂuencing NF-κB
Our previous data demonstrated that DMC inhibits mPGES-1
expression after stimulation of HeLa cells with IL-1β and TNFα by
interacting with EGR1 and NF-κB [6]. DMC inhibited thereby EGR1
expression and induced the translocation of NF-κB to the nucleus.
Even nuclear NF-κB bound to DNA containing NF-κB binding sites
the transactivation activity of NF-κB was inhibited. As the human
EGR1 promoter contains similar transcription factor binding sites
like the human mPGES-1 promoter we examined if inhibition of
EGR1 expression after DMC treatment is mediated by a repression
of the EGR1 promoter activity. Therefore, HeLa cells were transiently
transfected with the human EGR1 promoter-luciferase construct
(Fig. 1B), stimulated with IL-1β/TNFα and co-treated with increasing
concentrations of DMC or vehicle. Treatment of HeLa cells with IL-
1β/TNFα increased luciferase activity of the transfected EGR1 pro-
moter-luciferase construct about twofold. Simultaneous treatment
with increasing concentrations of DMC led to a dose-dependent, sig-
niﬁcant downregulation of luciferase activity of about 20% (Fig. 2A).
Next we mutated the NF-κB binding motif within the EGR1 promoter
construct (Fig. 1B) und transfected HeLa cells with this EGR1 promoter
mut NF-κB construct. As shown in Fig. 2B the inhibitory effect of DMC
after IL-1β/TNFα stimulation was abolished in the EGR1 promoter
mut NF-κB construct, indicating that the inhibitory effect of DMC on
the EGR1 promoter could be repealed by a mutation of the NF-κB bind-
ing motif.
3.2. DMC and NF-κB(p65)RelA
Next we determined which subunits of NF-κB (e.g. p50, p65) are
responsible for the inhibitory effect of DMC on EGR1 transcription.
Therefore, we transfected HeLa cells with 25 nM NF-κB(p65)RelA
siRNA or siRNA negative control and additionally with the EGR1 pro-
moter-luciferase construct, treated these cells with IL-1β/TNFα and
DMC and measured the luciferase activity. We observed that downre-
gulation of the EGR1 promoter activity by DMC could be prevented by
downregulation of NF-κB(p65)RelA protein expression using siRNA
(Fig. 3A). Inhibition of NF-κB(p65)RelA expression was veriﬁed by
western blot analysis (Fig. 3B).
3.3. DMC and HDAC1
After nuclear translocation NF-κB, especially (p65)RelA, associates
with several transcriptional co-activators or co-repressors such as
p300/CBP or HDAC1-3, respectively [8]. Especially binding of NF-κB
to HDAC1 is associated with inhibition of the transactivation activity
of NF-κB [16]. Therefore, we examined the effect of DMC on the
nuclear expression level of HDAC1 protein in HeLa cells after IL-
1β/TNFα treatment by western blot analysis. The nuclear protein
level of HDAC1 was not affected, neither by treatment of HeLa cells
with IL-1β/TNFα nor by co-treatment with DMC (Fig. 4A). Next we
investigated a possible inﬂuence of DMC on the HDAC activity. Using
a commercial available HDAC activity assay we detected a signiﬁcant
increase of HDAC activity in nuclear extract of IL-1β/TNFα stimulat-
ed and DMC treated HeLa cells in comparison to stimulated HeLa
cells without DMC treatment (Fig. 4B). Taken together we show
that DMC does not inﬂuence the nuclear HDAC1 expression level,
but the HDAC activity is enhanced under DMC inﬂuence.

3.5. HDAC1 and the EGR1 promoter in the presence of DMC
Since DMC treated cells formed a NF-κB(p65)RelA–HDAC1 complex
which binds to the EGR1 promoter dependent on the HDAC1 activity,
we treated stimulated HeLa cells, transfected with the EGR1 promoter-
luciferase construct, with DMC and siRNA against HDAC1. DMC treat-
ment inhibited EGR1 promoter activity induced by stimulation of
HeLa cells with IL-1β/TNFα. Repression of HDAC1 expression (Fig. 8A)
repealed the inhibitory effect of DMC on EGR1 promoter activity
(Fig. 8B). In conclusion, treatment of HeLa cells with DMC led to an en-
hanced formation of a complex consisting of NF-κB and HDAC1 that
bound to the EGR1 promoter responsible for downregulation of EGR1
expression. EGR1 downregulation seems to be the major effect of
DMC leading to transcriptional inhibition of mPGES-1.
4. Discussion
Our previous work demonstrated that treatment of IL-1β/TNFα
stimulated HeLa cells with DMC leads to a repression of mPGES-1
transcription and therefore to a reduced PGE2 production, that was
mainly due to a downregulation of EGR1 and interference with
NF-κB [1]. Downregulation of EGR1 after DMC treatment seems to
be the most important mechanism responsible for the observed re-
pression of mPGES-1, as we demonstrated that mPGES-1 expression
was fully reconstituted by EGR1 overexpression [1]. Here we describe,

that the transactivation activity of NF-κB is inhibited after treatment

The EGR1 promoter includes a NF-κB binding motif that is impor-
tant for the regulation of EGR1 [12]. After DMC treatment a complex
containing NF-κB(p65)RelA and HDAC1 binds to this NF-κB binding
motif and represses EGR1 transcription. Association of RelA with
HDAC1 is dependent on phosphorylation of two threonine residues
(T435 and T505) that could be phosphorylated by checkpoint kinase
1 (Chk1) and an unknown kinase [17–18]. Thus DMC may inﬂuence
these kinase activities. Another possibility is that DMC interacts di-
rectly with HDAC1. Until now several potential target proteins of
DMC have been proposed but a direct binding of DMC at these target
proteins could not be demonstrated [19]. We could show that DMC
intercalates in cellular membranes leading to changes in the physico-
chemical properties of the membrane [20]. Possibly due to this bio-
physical interplay DMC inﬂuences cellular signaling pathways.
DMC has been often compared to its chemical derivative cele-
coxib and it is obvious that both substances inﬂuence similar target
proteins [19]. Only the prostaglandin synthesis pathway seems to
be an exception as celecoxib inhibits COX-2-activity and mPGES-1
activity, whereas DMC inhibits mPGES-1 activity and expression
but has no effect on COX-2 activity or expression [6]. This is of inter-
est as activation of NF-κB is an important mechanism for the induc-
tion of COX-2 and mPGES-1 expression after various stimuli [21].
Nevertheless, COX-2 expression is regulated by further mechanisms
like additional transcription factors (like Wnt/β-catenin) [22], mRNA
stabilization mechanisms (HUR, CUG-binding protein 2, etc.) and trans-
lational regulation mechanisms [23]. So in humans mPGES-1 expression
can be affected independently of COX-2 expression which depicts that
selective inhibition of PGE2 production by transcriptional targeting
mPGES-1 is possible. This is of importance as COX-inhibition is related
to various side effects which limited the long term use of selective or
unselective COX-inhibitors in clinical treatment regimes. Nevertheless,
celecoxib as well as other COX-2 inhibitors (e.g. rofecoxib) had gotten
attention due to their beneﬁcial effects on various diseases like their
anti-tumorigenic effects in patients with familial adenomatous polypo-
sis (FAP) [24], as well as the prevention of experimental autoimmune
encephalomyelitis (EAE) [25–26]. Both effects are mediated by COX-
dependent and COX-independent mechanisms [15,25–27]. The COX-
dependent mechanisms are mainly ascribed to the inhibition of PGE2
production. Therefore, selective inhibition of PGE2 production may be
as useful as inhibition of the complete prostanoid synthesis. This is con-
ﬁrmed by several experiments using mPGES-1 (−/−) mice. These mice
showed less severe symptoms of EAE [28] as well as a reduced incidence
of intestinal tumorigenesis [29], indicating that selective inhibition of
mPGES-1 is a promising therapeutic target for multiple sclerosis and
cancer. Furthermore, also the formation of a complex between NF-κB
(p65)RelA and HDAC1 after DMC treatment could have a beneﬁcial ef-
fect on multiplesclerosis, as very recently it has been published that
complex formation and deacetylation of NF-κB(p65)RelA by HDAC1/2
is a critical mechanism for Schwann cell myelination [30]. NF-κB and
HDAC1/2 act thereby in a coordinate fashion to regulate transcriptional-
ly Schwann cell differentiation. Enhancing this effect, for example by
DMC treatment, may have a therapeutic beneﬁt for several diseases
where defects in myelin repair play a critical role.
5. Conclusion
In conclusion, we could demonstrate that treatment of cells with
DMC leads to the formation of a complex between NF-κB(p65)RelA
and HDAC1 that is mainly responsible for the inhibition of EGR1 tran-
scription and subsequently inhibition of mPGES-1 transcription and
PGE2 production. Inhibition of PGE2 production and complex formation
between NF-κB(p65)RelA and HDAC1 are important anti-carcinogenic
or EAE-protective mechanisms. Therefore, a substance like DMC, lacking
