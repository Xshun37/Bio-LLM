Regulation of the human Sox9 promoter by the CCAAT-binding factor
David C. Colter, Sonsoles Piera-Velazquez, David F. Hawkins, Mary Kate Whitecavage,
Sergio A. Jimenez, David G. Stokes*
Department of Medicine, Division of Rheumatology, Thomas Jefferson University, Jefferson Medical College,
233 South 10th Street, Room 511 BLSB, Philadelphia, PA 19107, USA
Received 8 February 2005; received in revised form 28 March 2005; accepted 5 April 2005
Abstract
Sox9 is an essential transcriptional regulator of chondrogenesis and chondrocyte-specific gene expression; however, the identity and
function of transcription factors that regulate Sox9 gene expression are not well understood. Here, we have undertaken an analysis of the
human Sox9 proximal promoter region in an effort to elucidate the function and identity of transcriptional regulators that are important for
controlling Sox9 gene transcription. By transfection analysis, we show that elements residing between 256 bp and +67 bp are important for
the overall level of Sox9 promoter activity. Previously, two CCAAT boxes were identified in the Sox9 mouse and human promoters (position
60 bp and 100 bp) by sequence analysis (Kanai, Y., Koopman, P., 1999. Structural and functional characterization of the mouse Sox9
promoter: implications for campomelic dysplasia. Hum. Mol. Genet., 8: 691–696). We demonstrate by electrophoretic mobility shift
(EMSA) competition and supershift assays that the CCAAT-binding factor (CBF) can form a complex with both Sox9 CCAAT boxes in
nuclear extracts from multiple cell lines. Transfection of human Sox9 promoter–luciferase constructs containing mutated or deleted CCAAT
boxes demonstrated that both CCAAT boxes are important for Sox9 promoter activity in chondrogenic cell lines and primary chondrocytes.
Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) experiments demonstrated that CBF interacts with the Sox9 promoter in vivo. Together, these studies
show that the Sox9 promoter is regulated by CBF through its interaction with two functional CCAAT boxes.

1. Introduction
Sox9 is an HMG DNA-binding domain transcription
factor that is expressed during skeletal development in
mesenchymal cells, pre-chondrocytes, and differentiated
chondrocytes but not in hypertrophic chondrocytes (Wright
et al., 1995; Healy et al., 1996; Ng et al., 1997; Mertin et al.,
1999). Sox9, in cooperation with other transcription factors
(such as L-Sox5 and Sox6), is a potent transcriptional
activator of the COL2A1 gene through the intronic enhancer
(Bell et al., 1997; Lefebvre et al., 1997, 1998; Ng et al.,
1997). Sox9 also controls the expression of other chon-
drocyte-specific extracellular matrix protein genes such as
aggrecan, COL11A2 and, as shown by our work, COL9A1
(Bridgewater et al., 1998; Sekiya et al., 2000; Zhang et al.,
2003). Pivotal work in mice has shown that Sox9 is essential
for chondrogenesis to proceed and is indeed required for
multiple steps along the chondrocyte differentiation path-
way. In mice that are chimeric for a knock-out of the Sox9
gene, cells that are Sox9/ do not take part in mesen-
chymal condensation and hence chondrogenesis, and they
do not express extracellular matrix genes known to be
activated by Sox9 in chondrocytes (Bi et al., 1999). Further
work in which the Sox9 gene was conditionally knocked-
out in undifferentiated limb bud mesenchymal cells or in
mesenchymal cells after undergoing condensation has
revealed that Sox9 is vital for several different steps of
chondrogenic differentiation to occur (Akiyama et al.,

which the most information exists include the fibroblast

primary chondrocytes and in the mesenchymal C3H10T1/2
cell line through a MAP kinase mediated pathway (Murakami
et al., 2000a). In contrast, IL-1h and TNF-a cause a dramatic
suppression of Sox9 gene expression in primary chondro-

(Murakami et al., 2000b). The suppression of Sox9 gene
transcription appears to be mediated through the direct/
indirect action of NF-nB (Murakami et al., 2000b). In certain
instances, BMP-2 and 4 have been shown to up-regulate Sox9

findings have shown that hedgehog protein family members
can up-regulate Sox9 expression, apparently through the
promoter region (Tavella et al., 2004).
There have been only a few studies reported concerning
the functional analysis of the Sox9 promoter. In one, Kanai
and Koopman (1999) compared the sequence of the mouse
and human Sox9 proximal promoter regions and performed
some functional analyses of the mouse promoter. The
sequence analysis revealed conserved transcription factor
binding sites for GATA, CREB and two sites for CBF/NF-Y,
along with a possible SOX/Sry binding site. A DNaseI
hypersensitive site within the mouse Sox9 proximal
promoter region (approximately between position 200 to
70 bp) was observed in gonadal somatic cells, undiffer-
entiated C3H10T1/2 cells and L-3T3 cells, but not in liver
cells. The mouse Sox9 proximal promoter activity was also
assessed in C3H10T1/2 and L-3T3 cells, and in primary
testes, ovary and liver cells. Promoter activity was high in
C3H10T1/2 and L-3T3 cells until a deletion past 193 bp
relative to the transcriptional start site was made, similar to
our results with the human Sox9 promoter in which activity
decreased markedly when a deletion was made past position
172 bp (Fig. 1). However, cells of the chondrocytic
lineage or cells undergoing chondrogenesis in vitro were not
investigated in this work.
More recently, Morishitu et al. (Morishita et al., 2001)
reported on the activity of the human Sox9 promoter region
in several different cell types, including rat articular
chondrocytes (RACs), rat chondrosarcoma cells (RCS),
ATDC5 cells, C3H10T1/2 cells and MC3T3-E1 cells. Their
work showed that promoter activity decreased as much as 6-
fold when deletions were made past 1453 bp up to 113
bp in RACs and approximately 3–5-fold in RCS, ATDC5
and MC3T3-E1 cells. The highest activity was observed
with a promoter fragment starting at position 734 bp
relative to the transcriptional start site. Interestingly, in this
work, an enhancer element within the first intron was
reported to function mainly in undifferentiated ATDC5
cells, where it resulted in approximately an 8-fold increase
in promoter activity. The enhancer element was less active
in RACs and RCS cells, in which it resulted in a 2-fold
increase. The significance of this putative enhancer region is
not clear since the enhancer was efficient only in undiffer-
entiated ATDC5 cells. Nuclear factor binding to the
enhancer element was demonstrated by electromobility shift
assays (EMSA) and, combined with the promoter–reporter
experiments, showed that the enhancer consisted of a 30 bp
sequence that is conserved between mouse and human.
Despite the extensive studies reviewed above, transcription
factors that interact with the Sox9 proximal promoter region
and/or the upstream elements have yet to be identified and
functionally characterized. Here, we describe our studies on
the identification and functional analysis of transcription
factors that interact with the Sox9 proximal promoter. We
demonstrate in this work that the two CCAAT boxes found
within the human Sox9 proximal promoter region can
specifically bind to the CCAAT-binding factor and that
mutation or deletion of the CCAAT boxes diminishes Sox9
promoter activity.
2. Results
2.1. Deletion analysis of the human Sox9 promoter
In order to determine which regions of the human Sox9
proximal promoter are important for transcriptional activity,
we cloned a 1.1 kb fragment encompassing the region from
1034 to +67 bp relative to the transcriptional start site (+1)
into the pGL3-luciferase reporter vector. Sequence analysis
of this region predicts a number of relevant transcription
factor binding sites, including one Sry site, two yEF1 sites,
one NFAT site, adjacent NF-nB, cREL and Sp1 sites, a one-
half CREB site and two CCAAT sites (Fig. 1A top) (Kanai
and Koopman, 1999). A series of 5V-deletion constructs
(Fig. 1A bottom) were prepared and the constructs were
tested for promoter activity in the mouse mesenchymal cell
line C3H10T1/2, the embryonal carcinoma ATDC5 cell line
and in fetal bovine epiphyseal chondrocytes. In C3H10T1/2
cells, we observed that the activity of the Sox9 promoter
decreased by ¨35% when a deletion was introduced up to
position 839 bp and that the activity remained at this level
through a deletion to position 534 bp (Fig. 1B). When a
deletion to position 256 bp was introduced, promoter
activity rose to levels observed for the full-length 1034/
+67 bp construct (Fig. 1B). A further deletion to position
218 bp reduced Sox9 promoter activity to approximately
60–80% of the 1034/+67 bp construct (Fig. 1B). A
dramatic reduction in promoter activity (33% of the 1034/
+67 construct), however, was observed when a deletion to
122 was introduced (Fig. 1B). When a deletion construct
to position 172 bp was tested, the Sox9 promoter activity
was similarly reduced to approximately 25–30% of the full
length 1034/+67 bp construct (Fig. 1C). Fig. 1D shows
that the deletion constructs yielded similar results when
tested in the chondrocytic ATDC5 cell line. Lastly, when the
full length 1034/+67 bp construct, the 534 bp construct
and the 172 bp and 122 bp constructs were transfected
into fetal bovine epiphyseal chondrocytes, the relative

also similar to what was observed in the chondrogenic cell

within the region extending from 256 bp to the transcrip-
tional start site, confirming previous results reported for the
mouse and human Sox9 promoters (Kanai and Koopman,

2.2. CBF binds to the CCAAT-1 box in the human Sox9
proximal promoter
Based on a sequence comparison of the Sox9 proximal
promoter regions from mouse, chick and human, Kanai and
Koopman (1999) predicted binding sites for CREB and
CBF, among others, within the region from 172 bp to +1
bp (Fig. 2). In order to determine which cis-elements within
the Sox9 proximal promoter region are important for
transcriptional activity, we investigated whether the
CCAAT-1 box (Fig. 2) within the Sox9 proximal promoter
specifically interacted with CBF/NF-Y in nuclear extracts
from chondrocytic cell lines. Shown in Fig. 3 are the results
of a competition and supershift EMSA for CBF/NF-Y
binding. Fig. 3A shows that specific complexes form with a
probe containing the wild type (WT) Sox9 CCAAT-1 box
(Fig. 3D) in nuclear extracts from ATDC5 cells, C3H10T1/2
cells, HTB human chondrosarcoma cells, NIH3T3 cells and
RCS rat chondrosarcoma cells. Interestingly, two closely
spaced DNA–protein complexes were observed in ATDC5,
C3H10T1/2 and NIH3T3 cells but not in HTB or RCS cells
(Fig. 3A). In Fig. 3B, the results of a competition analysis
with WT Sox9 and consensus and mutated CCAAT box
probes (sequences shown in Fig. 3D), and ATDC5 cell
nuclear extract are presented. In this experiment, both
complexes that formed with the WT Sox9 CCAAT-1 box
probe were efficiently competed away with 100X unlabeled
WT Sox9 CCAAT-1 box probe (lane 5, Fig. 3B). However,
only the stronger, upper complex was competed with the
unlabeled consensus CCAAT box probe (lane 6, Fig. 3B).
Neither complex was competed with the unlabeled mutated
consensus CCAAT box probe (lane 7, Fig. 3B). Lane 8 in
Fig. 3B shows that the labeled consensus CCAAT box probe
exhibited a DNA–protein complex identical to the upper
complex formed with the WT Sox9 CCAAT-1 box probe.
Finally, lane 10 in Fig. 3B shows that 100X unlabeled WT
Sox9 CCAAT-1 box probe efficiently competed with the
complex formed on the labeled consensus CCAAT box
probe. In order to further determine the identity of the
factor(s) binding to the Sox9 CCAAT-1 box, a supershift
analysis was employed. Shown in Fig. 3C is a supershift
analysis of the WT Sox9 CCAAT-1 box with anti-CBF-B
antibody. As can be seen, the anti-CBF-B antibody inhibited
CBF binding to both the WT Sox9 CCAAT-1 probe and to
the CCAAT consensus probe in nuclear extracts from
ATDC5, C3H10T1/2 and RCS cells.

We next investigated whether CBF could interact with
the Sox9 CCAAT-1 and CCAAT-2 boxes if they were
present on the same DNA probe. We used PCR to generate a
95 bp probe that encompassed the region containing both
Sox9 CCAAT boxes. We show in Fig. 5A that when the
larger probe containing both Sox9 CCAAT boxes is used in
an EMSA with ATDC5 cell nuclear extracts, two complexes
are formed. The lower complex has the same mobility as
that of a single Sox9 CCAAT box bound by CBF, whereas
the other, more slowly moving complex, is presumably
caused by CBF binding to both sites at once. We verified
this assumption by performing a cross competition analysis
shown in Fig. 5A and B. In Fig. 5A, competition with
probes containing either a single WT Sox9 CCAAT-1 box or
a single WT Sox9 CCAAT-2 box effectively competed
binding to the double Sox9 CCAAT box probe, as did the
double Sox9 CCAAT box probe itself. In Fig. 5B, we show
that WT single Sox9 CCAAT box probes can compete with
this binding as can a consensus CCAAT box probe. We also
show that neither mutated Sox9 CCAAT-1 or CCAAT-2 box
probes, nor a mutated consensus CCAAT box probe can
compete with the Sox9 WT double CCAAT box probe.
Lastly, we show in Fig. 5B that inclusion of an anti-CBF-B
antibody inhibits binding of CBF to the Sox9 double
CCAAT box probe as it did to the single Sox9 CCAAT box
probes (Figs. 3C and 4B).
2.4. The Sox9 CCAAT boxes contribute to promoter activity
In order to determine whether the CCAAT boxes within
the Sox9 promoter contribute to promoter activity, a deletion
of the CCAAT-1 sequence and a mutation of the CCAAT-2
box were introduced into the full length 1034/+67 bp
Sox9 promoter–luciferase construct (Fig. 6D). Fig. 6A
shows that when the Sox9 CCAAT-1 box is deleted
(CCAAT-1d) and the CCAAT-2 box is mutated (CCAAT-
2m), Sox9 promoter activity decreases to between 30 and
50% of the activity of the WT Sox9 promoter in C3H10T1/2
cells. Fig. 6B shows that deletion of the CCAAT-1 box
reduces Sox9 promoter activity by approximately 50% in
ATDC5 cells. In Fig. 6C, we demonstrate that deletion of
the CCAAT-1 box and mutation of the CCAAT-2 box reduce
Sox9 promoter activity by 50–60% in fetal bovine
chondrocytes. Finally, in Fig. 6D, we show that a construct
containing both the deleted CCAAT-1 box and the mutated
CCAAT-2 box exhibits a lower transcriptional activity than
the constructs containing either the deleted CCAAT-1 box or
the mutated CCAAT-2 box alone in C3H10T1/2 cells.
2.5. Association of CBF with the Sox9 promoter in vivo
In order to demonstrate that CBF interacts with the Sox9
promoter in vivo, a chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP)
assay was carried out. In this procedure, DNA-binding
proteins are covalently linked to the genomic DNA by
exposure to a cross-linking agent and then the DNA–
protein complexes are isolated by immunoprecipitation.
After reversal of cross-links, the DNA is subjected to PCR
with primers that encompass the transcription factor binding
site of interest. C3H10T1/2 cells were subjected to cross-
linking with formaldehyde, the cells were lysed and the
chromatin was sheared by sonication. The chromatin was
then immunoprecipitated with anti-CBF-B antibodies and
the resulting DNA was subjected to PCR amplification with
primers flanking the region containing the two Sox9
CCAAT boxes (Fig. 7A). Fig. 7B shows that anti-CBF-B
antibodies specifically enriched for the region containing the
Sox9 proximal promoter, as did an anti-TFIIB antibody but
not a non-specific IgG antibody (control antibody, Fig. 7B).

tional CCAAT boxes (CCAAT-1 and CCAAT-2) and that
CBF can specifically interact with both CCAAT boxes. We
also demonstrate that deletion or mutation of these CCAAT
boxes diminishes promoter activity. The CCAAT box,
bound most commonly by the heterotrimeric CCAAT-box
binding factor, also known as Nuclear Factor-Y, is one of
several common elements found in the promoters of
eukaryotic genes (25% of eukaryotic promoters) (Maity
and de Crombrugghe, 1998; Mantovani, 1999). CCAAT
boxes are usually found between 60 and 100 bp of the
transcriptional start site, can be in the sense or anti-sense
(inverted) orientation, and multiple functional CCAAT
boxes within a promoter region are not uncommon. CCAAT
boxes operate at multiple levels in the transcriptional control
of genes. Interaction of CBF with the CCAAT box can
promote recruitment of the basal transcriptional machinery
through interactions with subunits of TFIID (Bellorini et al.,
1997; Frontini et al., 2002). Mutation of the CCAAT box
can cause a decrease in the overall level of transcription
whereas in other cases, CBF-CCAAT box interaction, along
with other transcription factors, is necessary for induction of
transcription by agents such as serum and steroid hormones
(Kennedy et al., 1990; Framson and Bornstein, 1993;
Subramanian et al., 1998; Scholz et al., 1999; Sorensen
and Wintersberger, 1999; Jump et al., 2001). CBF binding
to the CCAAT box has also been shown to reorganize
chromatin structure around the promoters of certain genes.
Interaction of CBF with DNA results in significant DNA-
bending, CBF can interact with CCAAT boxes that are
bound by nucleosomes and binding of CBF to the CCAAT
box can result in the prevention of nucleosome interactions
at the HSP70 and topoisomerase IIa promoters (Land-
sberger and Wolffe, 1995; Ronchi et al., 1995; Li et al.,
1998; Motta et al., 1999; Coustry et al., 2001).
CBF plays an important role in the regulation of cell
proliferation by interacting with the promoters of several
cell-cycle related genes. Indeed, CBF, usually through
interactions with multiple CCAAT boxes, regulates several
genes involved in cell proliferation and control of the cell
cycle, such as topoisomerase IIa, thymidine kinase, E2F1
and cdc25c (Zwicker et al., 1995; Good and Chen, 1996;
Isaacs et al., 1996; Kim et al., 1997; Chang et al., 1999;
Wang et al., 1999; Adachi et al., 2000; Hu and Maity, 2000;
Manni et al., 2001; Magan et al., 2003). Over-expression of
a dominant-negative CBF in fibroblasts inhibits cell growth
and a reduction in the level of the CBF-B subunit has been
observed upon serum deprivation in several cell lines
(Farina et al., 1999; Hu and Maity, 2000). Mice in which
the CBF-B subunit gene was knocked out die in early
embryogenesis and mouse embryo fibroblasts derived from

proliferation and increased apoptosis (Bhattacharya et al.,

differentiate into skeletal muscle cells, CCAAT binding
activity is lost as a result of a dramatic down-regulation of

The two Sox9 CCAAT boxes, one found at 60 bp, the
other at 100 bp, may play multiple roles in regulating
Sox9 promoter activity. The placement of the CCAAT boxes
within the Sox9 promoter suggests that CBF might be
involved in all of the aforementioned transcriptional
regulatory roles with regard to Sox9 gene expression
(cooperative interactions, interaction with basal transcrip-
tional machinery, modification of chromatin structure). For
instance, the proximal CCAAT-2 box (60 bp) may be
involved in interactions with the general transcriptional
machinery while the distal CCAAT-1 box (100 bp) could
be responsible, at least in part, for setting up the open
chromatin configuration found in this region of the Sox9
promoter and/or be involved in interactions with other
upstream regulatory factors. However, it is certainly
possible that both CCAAT boxes participate in all of the
regulatory roles discussed above. In the case of the human
Sox9 gene, the two CCAAT boxes are located 41 bp apart
on center as shown in Fig. 2, while in the mouse, they are 46
bp on center, equating to approximately 4 turns of the helix.
Since the CCAAT boxes are inverted relative to each other,
the Sox9 CCAAT boxes are most likely on opposite faces of
the DNA helix. The Sox9 CCAAT box placement, therefore,
has the potential to impact on the structure of the promoter

nucleosomal interactions with DNA.

performed by Wunderle et al. (1998) in which human YAC

expression was lost in the developing long bones. These
studies indicated that elements controlling the expression of

the upstream region of the Sox9 gene in mouse, human and
the Japanese puffer fish (Fugu rubripes), five small regions
of conserved sequence, denoted E1–E5, were found to
reside between 28 and 290 kb upstream of both the mouse
and human genes (Bagheri-Fam et al., 2001). Elements E1
and E2 lie in the regions that may control expression in the
neuroectoderm and developing ear, whereas elements E3–
E5 lie in the region implicated in controlling limb and
vertebral column expression (Bagheri-Fam et al., 2001).

therefore, be involved in chondrogenesis and chondrocyte
maturation at multiple steps. For example, chondrocytes

show little cell division and exhibit senescence during

4. Materials and methods
4.1. Cell culture
C3H10T1/2 cells, NIH3T3 cells and HTB94 (HTB)
human chondrosarcoma cells were obtained from ATCC.
RCS cells were a kind gift from Dr. Benoit de Crombrugghe
(Mukhopadhyay et al., 1995). ATDC5 cells were obtained
from the RIKEN BioResource Center Cell Bank (Ibaraki,
Japan). C3H10T1/2 and RCS cells were maintained in
Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) containing
10% heat inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS), supple-
mented with l-glutamine (2 mM), penicillin (100 units/ml)
and streptomycin (100 Ag/ml). NIH3T3 cells were main-
tained in DMEM containing 10% bovine calf serum and
supplements as above. HTB cells were maintained in
DMEM containing 10% FBS and supplements as above.
ATDC5 cells were propagated in DMEM/Ham’s F-12 (1:1)
containing 5% FBS, 10 Ag/ml human transferrin, 3108
M sodium selenite and supplements as above.
Bovine fetal epiphyseal cartilage from the knee was
obtained from Animal Technologies, Inc. (Tyler, TX).
Chondrocytes were isolated as previously described (Coim-
bra et al., 2004). After isolation, fetal bovine chondrocytes
(FBCs) were frozen in 90% FBS/10% DMSO until future
use. Upon thawing, FBCs were maintained in DMEM
containing 10% FBS, supplemented with l-glutamine (2
mM), penicillin (100 units/ml), streptomycin (100 Ag/ml),
fungizone (2.5 Ag/ml) and ascorbic acid (50 Ag/ml). FBCs
were cultured at a density of 5106 cells in 60 mm plastic
petri dishes that had been previously coated with 0.9 ml of a
10% solution of poly-(2-hydroxyethyl) methacrylate (poly-
HEMA, Poly Sciences Inc., Malvern, PA) to prevent
attachment as described previously (Reginato et al., 1994).
4.2. Sox9-luciferase constructs
The human Sox9 proximal promoter region from 1034
bp to +67 bp relative to the transcriptional start site was
obtained by PCR using human genomic DNA (a kind gift
from Dr. Charlene Williams) as a template and the following
primers: forward; 5V-GTGGAGCGTTTTGTCTGC-3V,
reverse; 5V-TGAAACTGGCGAGTCTCC-3V. The PCR pro-
duct was cloned into the pCR-Blunt vector (Invitrogen,
Carlsbad, CA) and then transferred as a KpnI–EcoRV frag-
ment into the pGL3-Basic vector (Promega, Madison, WI)
that had been digested with KpnI–SmaI. The various 5V-
Sox9 promoter–deletion constructs were made by digesting
the parent Sox9 promoter–luciferase construct (1034/+67,
Fig 1A) with KpnI (generates an ExoIII protected 3V-
overhang) and SpeI (generates an ExoIII digestable 5V-
overhang) followed by Exonuclease III treatment (Erase-a-
base kit, Promega). The Sox9 promoter–luciferase CCAAT-
1 deletion construct was made with the ExSite PCR-based
site-directed mutagenesis kit from Stratagene (La Jolla, CA)
and the following primers: CCAAT-1 box deletion; forward,
5V-CCCCAAAATCGGGTCAGCTGCCTGCC-3V, and
reverse, GGCAGGCAGCTGACCCGATTTTGGGG. The
CCAAT-2 box mutation was introduced by a two-step
PCR-based mutagenesis procedure (Genuario et al., 1993)
with the following primers: CCAAT-2 box mutation;
forward, 5V-TGTGTTCGGCGGGTGGCTCTA-3V and
reverse, 5V-TAGAGCCACCCGCCGAACACA-3V. The
double CCAAT-1+2 mutation/deletion construct was made
by transferring a PvuII–PstI fragment containing the
CCAAT-2 mutation into the CCAAT-1 deleted Sox9 promo-
ter construct. All constructs were verified by sequencing.
4.3. Nuclear extracts and electrophoretic mobility shift
assays
Nuclear extracts were prepared from sub-confluent cells
according to the method of Dignam et al. (1983) using the
CellLytic NuCLEAR extraction kit (Sigma-Aldrich, St.
Louis, MO). Briefly, cells were placed in hypotonic buffer
(10 mM HEPES [pH 7.9], 1.5 mM MgCl2, 10 mM KCl and
0.5 mM DTT) and incubated on ice for 15 min. Igeapal CA-
630 was added to a final concentration of 0.6% and the
mixture was vortexed vigorously for 10 s. Nuclei were
recovered by centrifugation at 3300 g for 30 s at 4 -C and
extracted in buffer containing 20 mM HEPES pH 7.9, 0.42
M NaCl, 25% glycerol, 1.5 mM MgC12, 0.2 mM EDTA and
0.5 mM DTT for 30 min at 4 -C by gentle shaking. The
extract was then centrifuged for 15 min at 25,000 g, and
the supernatant was snap frozen at 70 -C. All buffers
contained a protease inhibitor cocktail (2 mM 4-(2-amino-
ethyl) benzenesulfonylfluoride, 1.4 pM trans-epoxysuc-
cinyl-l-leucylamido [4-guanidinobutane], 130 pM bestatin,
1 AM leupeptin and 0.3 pM aprotinin; Sigma-Aldrich).
Electromobility shift assays (EMSAs) were carried out as
previously described with minor modifications (Stokes and
Perry, 1995; Stokes et al., 2001; Zhang et al., 2003). Briefly,
binding reactions consisted of 12.5 mM Hepes, pH 7.9, 50–
100 mM NaCl, 5% glycerol, 2 mg/ml BSA, 1–2 Ag poly-
dIdC, 0.1 mM EDTA, 0.1 mM DTT, 0.5–1 ng of 32P-end
labeled double stranded oligonucleotide probes (20–35 bp)
or 4–5ng end labeled PCR-generated probe (95 bp) and
10–15 Ag of nuclear protein. Binding reactions were
incubated for 30 min at RT and then loaded onto either
5% acrylamide-0.5X tris–borate-EDTA gels (95 bp
CCAAT-1+2 probe) or 50 mM Tris/0.38 M glycine-2 mM
EDTA gels (double stranded oligonucleotide single
CCAAT-box probes) and electrophoresed at 130 V for 2–
3 h. For competition analyses, 100-fold excess of unlabeled-
competitor probe was included in the binding reaction. For
the supershift experiments, 1–2 Al of anti-CBF-B antibody
(Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Santa Cruz, CA) was added to
the binding reaction for 25 min at room temperature (RT)
D.C. Colter et al. / Matrix Biology 24 (2005) 185–197
194


prior to the addition of labeled DNA probe. After addition
of labeled DNA-probe, the binding reaction was incubated
for an additional 20 min at RT.
4.4. Transient transfection and luciferase assays
All cell types were transfected with the FuGENE 6
transfection reagent (Roche, Indianapolis, IN). C3H10T1/2
cells were plated at 50,000 cells/well and ATDC5 cells at
100,000 cells/well in 6-well plates 24 h before transfection.
Cell lines were transfected with 2 Ag DNA/well (1.5 Ag
construct and 0.5 Ag pCMVh as a control for transfection
efficiency). FBCs were transfected utilizing the procedure of
Madry and Trippel et al. (Madry and Trippel, 2000), with
modifications. FBCs were maintained in polyHEMA coated
dishes in suspension culture in order to preserve the
differentiated phenotype (Reginato et al., 1994; Stokes et
al., 2002). FBCs in suspension culture were digested
overnight with 0.5 mg/ml collagenase in DMEM containing
10% FBS and supplements. After digestion, the cells were
plated into 24-well plates at 75,000/well and grown to
¨70% confluence. The FBCs were then treated with 10 U/
ml hyaluronidase for 4 h before and during transfection.
FBCs were transfected with 1 Ag DNA/well (0.5 Ag
construct and 0.5 Ag pCMVh). Cells were harvested 36–
42 h after transfection and luciferase assays were performed
with a luciferase assay kit (Promega) and a Turner Designs
TD 20/20 luminometer (Turner Designs, Sunnyvale CA). h-
galactosidase assays were performed spectrophotometrically
with a h-galactosidase enzyme assay system (Promega).
Protein concentrations in the cell lysates were determined
using the commassie-blue protein assay (Pierce, Rockford,
IL). Luciferase activity was normalized to both h-galacto-
sidase activity and protein concentration. Transfection data
represent at least three independent experiments each
performed in triplicate.
4.5. Chromatin immunoprecipitation assay
The chromatin immunoprecipitation assay was performed
using the Chip-IT kit (Active Motif, Carlsbad, CA) as per
manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, three 10 cm2 plates of
70–80%-confluent C3H10T1/2 cells were treated with 1%
formaldehyde in PBS for 10 min at RT. The formaldehyde
was inactivated by the addition of 0.125 M glycine in PBS to
the cells for 5 min at RT. The cells were then washed in ice-
cold PBS and harvested by scraping. The cells were then
resuspended in lysis buffer and the nuclei released by
Dounce homogenization. The nuclei were pelleted by
centrifugation at 2400 g for 10 min at 4 -C. To shear
genomic DNA, the nuclei were then sonnicated on ice with
fifteen 20 s pulses using a Vibra Cell sonnicator (Sonics and
Materials, Inc., Danbury, CT) set at 30% output. The sheared
DNA was then centrifuged at 12,000 g for 12 min at 4 -C
and the supernant collected. For each immunoprecipitation,
50 Al of chromatin was pre-cleared with Protein G agarose
beads for 1–2 h at 4 -C. One to three Ag of the appropriate
antibody was incubated with a pre-cleared chromatin aliquot
overnight at 4 -C with gentle rotation. The antibody-
chromatin mixtures were then incubated with Protein G
beads for 1.5 h at 4 -C. The antibody/chromatin/Protein G
bead mixtures were then washed extensively and the DNA
eluted. Crosslinks were reversed and the DNA was purified
for PCR analysis. Primers flanking the two CCAAT boxes
in the mouse Sox9 promoter were employed (see Fig. 7A).
The sequences of the primers utilized in Fig. 7C that
amplify a 153 bp segment approximately 3 kb upstream of
the Sox9 transcriptional start site were forward: 5V-
CAGAACTGACCGCAAGCAACACG-3V and reverse: 5V-
CACTGCAATTTTCAGCAACATCC-3V.
