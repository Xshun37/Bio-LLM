ABSTRACT
Background. RUNX3 is a major growth regulator of
gastric epithelial cells that is involved in gastric tumori-
genesis in both humans and mice. In this study, we
investigated the involvement of RUNX3 in tumor pro-
gression, and in the prognosis of human gastric cancer.
Methods. We analyzed the extent of RUNX3 protein
expression by immunohistochemistry in 95 primary gastric
adenocarcinomas, and correlated expression levels with
clinicopathological parameters. We examined the effects of
pFlag/RUNX3 on cell growth, apoptosis, and caspase-3
expression in AGS and SNU1 gastric cancer cell lines by
colony-forming assay, terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase
(TdT)-mediate deoxyuridine triphosphatase (dUTP) nick-end
labeling (TUNEL) assay, and Western blot analysis, respec-
tively. The pFlag/RUNX3 effects on AGS invasion and
migration potentials were also evaluated.
Results. RUNX3 expression was lost in 37 (39%) cases of
gastric cancer. The expression of RUNX3 in diffuse- and
mixed-type cancers was less frequent than expression in
intestinal-type cancer (P \ 0.001 and P = 0.001, respec-
tively). In addition, the loss of RUNX3 expression was
associated with lymph node metastasis (P = 0.02), and cor-
related with poor gastric cancer survival (P = 0.018). The
growth of gastric cancer cells was suppressed after pFlag/
RUNX3 transfection. The re-expression of RUNX3 resulted
in the upregulation of caspase-3 and promoted apoptosis.
Furthermore, Re-expression of RUNX3 induced signiﬁcant
inhibitions of AGS cell invasion and migration in vitro.
Conclusions. This work shows that loss of RUNX3
expression is highly associated with lymph node metastasis
and poor prognosis of gastric cancer. The re-expression of
RUNX3 may induce apoptosis and inhibit the growth as
well as invasion/migration of cancer cells. These results
indicate that the targeting of the RUNX3 pathway could
represent a potential modality for treating gastric cancer.
Gastric cancer is one of the leading causes of cancer death
throughout the world, and it often presents with a dismal
prognosis.1 Tumorigenesis of gastric cancer is attributable to
the
interactions
between
environmental
and
genetic
factors.2–5 In addition to dietary factors, the relationship
between gastric carcinoma and Helicobacter pylori has
attracted increased attention.6–9 Many genetic and epige-
netic alterations in oncogenes, tumor suppressor genes, cell-
cycle regulators, cell adhesion molecules, DNA repair genes,
and genetic instability as well as telomerase activation are
known to participate in the genesis or progression of gastric
cancer. A more thorough understanding of gastric carcino-
genesis is vital to improving diagnosis and treatment.10
Gastric cancer can be divided into two distinct histo-
logical subtypes: intestinal and diffuse.11 These subtypes
differ not only in morphology, but also in their clinical
characteristics and spectrum of genetic changes.12 Over-
expression of p53 and c-ErbB-2 is more common in early
intestinal
than
in
early
diffuse
gastric
cancer.13,14

Additionally, loss of heterozygosity of adenomatous pol-

Met and loss of E-cadherin expression occur more com-

Several recent studies have suggested that RUNX3 is a
novel tumor suppressor of gastric cancer.17,18 Human
RUNX genes encode the a-subunit of the Runt domain, an
evolutionarily conserved region of 128 amino acids which
is required for DNA binding and heterodimerization with
the b-subunit PEBP2/CBF.19 RUNX3 genes are crucially
involved in the proliferation and differentiation of human
cells.20 Recent investigations have established that RUNX3
participates in the tumorigenesis of gastric epithelium.
RUNX3 is frequently hypermethylated and downregulated
in human gastric carcinomas.18 Promoter hypermethylation
seems to be the main mechanism responsible for abolishing
the function of RUNX3 in cancer.21 However, mutations
that affect the RUNX3 DNA-binding ability and subcel-
lular mislocalization of the protein may also contribute to
the
RUNX3-related
gastric
tumor
development.22,23
Transforming growth factor b1 (TGF-b1) is known to
arrest cell growth by activating CDKN1A (p21WAF1/Cip1),
which encodes a cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor.24
RUNX3 is required for the TGF-b1-dependent induction of
the expression of p21 in gastric epithelial cells.24 RUNX3-
R122C, a mutation identiﬁed in a gastric cancer patient,
prevented activation of the p21 promoter. Moreover, the
gastric mucosa of RUNX3-null mice develop gastric epi-
thelial hyperplasia because of increased cell proliferation
and suppressed apoptosis, accompanied by a reduced sen-
sitivity to TGF-b1.20 Targeted restoration of RUNX3
expression in gastric cancer cells inhibited tumor growth
and prevented metastasis in nude mice.24
Several studies have suggested that RUNX3 exhibits bio-
logical and genetic characteristics that are typical of a tumor
suppressor gene, and that it may have an important role in the
progression of cancer. In this study, we investigated the
relationship between RUNX3 expression and clinicopatho-
logical characteristics and prognoses in gastric cancer
patients. We also evaluated the effects of RUNX3 re-expres-
sion on the proliferation and apoptosis of gastric cancer cells.
PATIENTS AND METHODS
Patients’ Clinicopathological Data and Sample
Preparation
A total of 95 patients with primary gastric adenocarci-
noma were enrolled in this study. These patients underwent
subtotal or total gastrectomy in the Kaohsiung Veterans
General Hospital, Kaohsiung, Taiwan between July 1995
and July 1997. Samples used in this study were obtained
with the approval of the Committee for Ethical Review of
Research Involving Human Subjects at Kaohsiung Veter-
ans General Hospital. None of the patients received
chemotherapy before surgical operation. Clinical informa-
tion was obtained from medical records. Samples were
taken from the representative cancerous lesions and from
adjacent noncancerous tissues. Clinical data on gender,
age, operation methods, extent of lymph node involvement,
and cancer recurrence were obtained from medical records
and data on depth of tumor invasion, location, tumor size,
histological differentiation, and Lauren classiﬁcation were
obtained from pathologic reports. Samples were handled
according to the guidelines of the Japanese Research
Society for Gastric Cancer, and written informed consent
was obtained from each patient before tissue acquisition.25
Tissues were ﬁxed in 10% buffered formalin, embedded in
parafﬁn, and sectioned. The sections were stained with a
hematoxylin and eosin stain and assessed by pathologists
without knowledge of clinical data. Gastric cancer was
classiﬁed according to Lauren’s criteria.11
Recombinant RUNX3 Protein Puriﬁcation
and Antibody Production
A recombinant protein with the C-terminal fragment of
RUNX3 was cloned into pET vector (abbreviated pET-
RX3C’) and puriﬁed by sodium dodecyl sulfate poly-
acrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE). A gel slice
containing RX3C0 was used to immunize rabbits as previ-
ously described, to generate speciﬁc antibodies recognizing
human RUNX3 protein.26 New Zealand white rabbits were
used to prepare polyclonal antibodies. On day 1, 1 ml
complete Freund’s adjuvant (CFA; Sigma, St. Louis, MO)
plus antigen (6:4) was injected into multiple sites, on
shaved areas adjacent to the vertebral column. Each
injection site received no more than 0.3 ml. On day 10, and
on every subsequent tenth day, the rabbits were boosted
with 1 ml incomplete Freund’s adjuvant (IFA; Sigma, St
Louis, MO) plus antigen (6:4), which was administered as
described for CFA treatment. After secondary boosts,
12 ml blood was collected every 3 days. The collected
blood was stood at room temperature overnight, and then
centrifuged to obtain sera. The anti-RUNX3 antibody was
obtained, and the speciﬁcity of antibody was tested by
Western blot analysis as described previously.26

Western Blot Analysis

solution containing 100 mM 2-mercaptoethanol, 2% SDS,
and 62.5 mM Tris-HCl (pH 6.7). Anti-caspase-3 antibodies
were purchased from Upstate (Lake Placid, NY).

In Vitro Invasion Assay
Methods for testing the invasion and migration capabil-
ities of tumor cells were performed in two-compartment
Boyden chambers (Neuro Probe Inc., USA). For invasion
assay, polycarbonate ﬁlters (8 lm pore size, 25 9 80 mm,
Neuro Probe Inc., USA) were precoated with 10 lg human
ﬁbronectin (Sigma, MO, USA) on the lower nonshiny side.
Fifty micrograms of ice-cold Matrigel (BD Biosciences,
CA) was diluted in 100 ll DMEM without fetal calf serum
(FCS) and then applied to the upper surface of the ﬁlters.
Thirty microliters of medium containing 10% FCS was
added to each well of the lower compartment of the cham-
ber. pFlag and pFlag/RUNX3 transfected cells were
harvested and resuspended in serum-free medium contain-
ing 0.1% bovine serum albumin, and 50 ll cell suspension
at the concentration of 3 9 105 cells/ml were added to each
well of the upper compartment. The cells were incubated for
16 h at 37C in 5% CO2. At the end of the incubation, ﬁlters
were ﬁxed in 100% methanol for 10 min and were then
stained with 10% Giemsa solution (Merck, Germany) for
1 h. Nonmigrating cells were completely removed by wip-
ing with cotton swabs. Cells that had invaded through the
pores to the lower surface of the ﬁlter were counted under
light microscope (200 9 , ten random ﬁelds from each
well). All experiments were performed in quadruplicate.

cells were allowed to reach 90–100% conﬂuence, and a

7.01 image editing software (Adobe, CA). The grid func-

wounded surface areas that were not covered by migrating
cells at each observation time point were counted and
tested against original wound surface areas. The result was
then statistically analyzed. The difference in areas repre-

covered
by

cells
immediately after wound inﬂiction or migrating cell repair
were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). All
experiments were performed in triplicate.
Statistical Analysis
Correlations between expression of RUNX3 and the
clinicopathological variables were analyzed by chi-square
test. Survival rates were calculated by Kaplan–Meier
method. Log-rank test was used for comparison of survival
curves. Also, the inﬂuence of each variable on survival was
assessed by the Cox proportional hazard model. For in vitro
studies, each experiment was done independently at least
twice with similar results. The data were compared by
Student’s t-test. SPSS software (version 11.05, SPSS, Inc.,
Chicago, IL) was used for the analyses. A P value less than
0.05 was regarded as statistically signiﬁcant.
RESULTS
RUNX3 Protein Expression in Gastric Cancer Tissue
Specimens
We analyzed the expression of RUNX3 protein in 95
gastric cancer tissues by immunohistochemistry. RUNX3
was strongly expressed in the nuclei of surface epithelial
cells of normal gastric mucosa (Fig. 1a, left). In gastric
cancer tissues, RUNX3 expression was negative (Fig. 1a,
right) in 37 (39%) cases, weakly positive in 40 (42%)
cases, and strongly positive (Fig. 1a, center) in 18 (19%)
cases. Table 1 summarizes the relationships between
RUNX3 expression and clinicopathological parameters.
Loss of RUNX3 expression was observed in 16.7% (10/37)
of intestinal-type cancers, 82.6% (19/37) of diffuse-type
cancers, and 66.7% (8/37) of mixed-type gastric cancers.
RUNX3 expression was more frequent in intestinal-type
cancer (P \ 0.001) and mixed-type cancer (P = 0.001)
than in diffuse-type cancer (Fig. 1a). Additionally, loss of
RUNX3 expression was associated with lymph node
metastasis (P = 0.02), and the frequencies of RUNX3

DISCUSSION
Runt domain family members are master regulators of
gene expression in major developmental pathways, and are
critically involved in cellular proliferation and differentia-
tion. The loss of RUNX3 expression is suggested to be
causally related to gastric cancer mainly because of the hy-
permethylation of the RUNX3 promoter.23 In this study, we
investigated the expression of RUNX3 protein in gastric
cancer tissues in 95 primary tumor samples by immunohis-
tochemistry. We found that RUNX3 was expressed in the
nuclei of surface epithelial cells in normal gastric mucosa,
but was not detectable in 37 of 95 (39%) primary tumors of
gastric cancer patients. This ﬁnding was consistent with
earlier reports, in which 30–60% of gastric cancer samples
exhibited loss of RUNX3 expression.18,23,29 The data indi-
cate that the perturbation of RUNX3 expression may be
involved in the multistep processes of gastric carcinogenesis.
Previous reports have shown that RUNX3 is required for
the TGF-b1-dependent induction of p21 expression in gas-
tric epithelial cells. TGF-b1 arrests cell growth by activating
p21, which encodes a cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor, and
this signal cascade is regarded as a tumor suppressor path-
way.30 The loss of RUNX3 expression may therefore
eliminate the ability of TGF-b1 to activate the p21 promoter,
and may lead to an imbalance between cell proliferation and
apoptosis.24 In this study, we detected a borderline associ-
ation between the loss of RUNX3 expression and advanced
tumor stages. Additionally, a striking association between
the loss of RUNX3 expression and lymph node metastasis
was observed (P = 0.02). Furthermore, the survival of
gastric cancer patients without RUNX3 expression was
signiﬁcantly worse than that of cancer patients with RUNX3
expression
(P = 0.018).

that

prognostic factor in gastric cancer. In our study, we showed
signiﬁcant inhibitions of invasion as well as migration in
AGS gastric cancer cells (Fig. 5). The data were also sup-
ported by experiments demonstrating that the enforced
restoration of RUNX3 expression led to downregulation of
cyclin D1, upregulation of apoptotic protein expression, and
abrogation of metastasis in animal models.29,30
