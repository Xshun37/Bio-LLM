Overexpression of ﬁbroblast growth factor receptors (FGFRs) has been observed in many types of human
tumors; however, the regulatory mechanism of human FGFR expression is still largely unknown. In the
present study, we ﬁrst identiﬁed the transcriptional initiation site in the human FGFR 1 gene by 5′-RACE.
Furthermore, we show that the expression of human FGFR 1 is regulated by E2F-1. Characterization of the
human FGFR 1 promoter demonstrated that two non-consensus E2F binding sequences at positions +4
to +22 and +25 to +43 relative to our identiﬁed transcriptional initiation site in the human FGFR 1 gene
were critical for E2F-1-mediated transactivation of human FGFR 1 promoter. Mutations of these sites
completely abolished the response of human FGFR 1 promoter to E2F-1 as well as E2F-1 binding in
electrophoretic mobility-shift assays. Furthermore, chromatin immunoprecipitation assay showed that E2F-1
was able to bind in vivo to the human FGFR 1 promoter. Moreover, human FGFR 1 protein expression was up-
regulated by the overexpression of E2F-1, but down-regulated by the overexpression of pRB in situ,
suggesting that the expression of human FGFR 1 is regulated by the pRB/E2F pathway. Because disruption of
the pRB/E2F pathway is frequently observed in tumor cells, our ﬁndings provide valuable information for
studying the role of FGFR 1 in tumor progression.

1. Introduction
Fibroblast growth factor receptors (FGFRs) are involved in many
biological processes during embryo development and homeostasis of
adult body tissues, and disruption of normal FGFR functions leads to
pathological conditions in humans. At present, the FGFR gene family
consists of four highly related genes, FGFR 1–4, encoding polypeptides
that are 55% to 72% identical in their amino acid sequence (Johnson
and Williams, 1993). In general, FGFR 1 and FGFR 2 genes exhibit
broad but distinct patterns of expression during development and in
adult tissues. On the other hand, FGFR 3 and FGFR 4 genes appear to
have more restricted expression patterns. Overexpression of FGFR 1
has been shown to contribute to the malignant proliferation of tumor
cells, including breast cancer (Adnane et al., 1991), prostate cancer
(Giri et al., 1999), pancreatic carcinoma (Ohta et al., 1995), and
melanoma (Yayon et al., 1997); however, the mechanisms of the
transcriptional regulation of FGFR 1 have not been clariﬁed.
Transcription factor E2F-1 (E2 promoter-binding factor 1) was
initially identiﬁed as a cellular DNA-binding factor required for E1A
(adenovirus 5 early region 1A product)-mediated activation of the
adenoviral E2 gene (Kovesdi et al., 1986). At present, E2F family
transcription factors are known to consist of eight genes, E2F-1 to E2F-
8, in mammals. Generally, E2F family members are divided into two
classes based on their transcriptional properties. E2F-1, E2F-2, and
E2F-3a are referred to as “activator” E2Fs because they transcription-
ally activate E2F target genes. Meanwhile, E2F-3b and E2F-4 to -8 are
referred to as “repressor” E2Fs because their main functions are as
transcriptional repressors of E2F target genes. E2F family members
share the highest homology in their DNA-binding domains, which is
consistent with the ﬁnding that each member binds to the E2F
consensus sequence that was originally identiﬁed as “TTTSSCGC” with
S=C or G in the adenovirus E2 gene promoter (Tao et al., 1997). Our
previous study showed that the transcription of mouse FGFR 1 was
stimulated by E2F-1 through the +28/+35 (“TTTGGCGG”) site
relative to the transcriptional initiation site (Tashiro et al., 2003).
Furthermore, we isolated a 1245 b.p. genomic fragment of human
FGFR 1 promoter and found that its transcription was also activated by
E2F-1; however, the human FGFR 1 promoter does not include the
sequence corresponding to the +28/+35 E2F-1 binding site in the
mouse FGFR 1 promoter; therefore, in the present study, we
investigated the mechanism of how E2F-1 activates the transcription
of human FGFR 1 promoter. We showed here that the expression of
the human FGFR 1 transcript is activated by E2F-1 through two non-
consensus E2F binding sequences at positions +4 to +22 and +25 to
+43 relative to the transcriptional initiation site in the human FGFR 1
gene. Moreover, we suggested that the expression of human FGFR 1 is
regulated by the pRB/E2F pathway.

2. Materials and methods
2.1. Cell culture
Human embryonic kidney HEK293T and HEK293 cells were
grown in Dulbecco's modiﬁed Eagle's medium (DMEM) supple-
mented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). Human ﬁbrosarcoma
(HT1080) cell line was grown in DMEM supplemented with 10%
FBS. Human colorectal (LoVo) and esophageal (EC17) carcinoma cell
lines were maintained in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with
5% FBS.
2.2. 5′-Rapid ampliﬁcation of cDNA ends (5′-RACE)
5′-RACE was performed using a 5′-Full Race Core Set (TaKaRaBio
Inc., Shiga, Japan). Total RNA from EC17 cells was reverse-transcribed
with a primer, 5′-TTT CAA ACT GAC CCT CGC TCG GCG-3′, speciﬁc to
exon 2 of human FGFR 1. First-round PCR was performed with a
forward primer, 5′-ACC AGC TCC GGC TCC ATT GTT CCC-3′, which
corresponds to +445 to +468 relative to the transcriptional initiation
site, and a reverse primer, 5′-GCA GCA CGG GTA CCG TGC GCC CTG CGG
GGC-3′, which corresponds to +258 to +287. Nested PCR was
performed with a forward primer, 5′-ACG CGG GCA CAC GCC CGC
TCG CAC AAG CCA-3′, which corresponds to +586 to +615, and a
reverse primer, 5′-GGA GAA AAG TCC TTG GGT TCC GCG-3′, which
corresponds to +218 to +241.
2.3. Reverse transcription-PCR (RT-PCR)
Total RNA was isolated from cells with TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen,
Carlsbad, CA, USA). Total RNA (2 μg) was reverse-transcribed with 2 μg
oligo(dT) primer using M-MLV reverse transcriptase (Promega,
Madison, MA, USA) at 42 °C for 1 h. For RT-PCR analysis, the
synthesized 1st-strand cDNA was analyzed by PCR with the primers
indicated in Table 1.
2.4. Plasmid construction and luciferase reporter assay
The −887/+60, −95/+60, and −95/+10 fragments of the
human FGFR 1 gene relative to the transcriptional initiation site were
ampliﬁed from EC17 genomic DNA by PCR and inserted into the vector
pGL3-basic (Promega, Madison, MA, USA). The primer sequences used
were as follows: for the −887/+60 fragment, 5′-CCG CTC GAG GCT
CTT GGC TCC TTC CTG GGA CAG-3′ (forward) and 5′-CCC AAG CTT GGC

3′ (reverse); for the −95/+10 fragment, 5′-CCG CTC GAG CGG AAC
TAC AAG GCC CAG CAG GC-3′ (forward) and 5′-CCG CTC GAG CGG AGC
GCT ATG CGC CGG CGA GGC GCG AGG G-3′ (reverse). Site-directed
mutant constructs were generated based on the −95/+60 construct
and the primer sequences used were as follows: for the −95/+60
MT1 fragment, 5′-ATA GCG CTC AGC ACG CTC TTG C-3′ (forward) and
5′-GCA AGA GCG TGC TGA GCG CTA T-3′ (reverse); for the −95/+60
MT2 fragment, 5′-TTG CGG CCA CAG ACT AGA CGT CCT C-3′ (forward)

CCT C-3′) and the reverse primer (5′-GAG GAC GTC TAG TCT GTG GCC
GCA A-3′) were used.
For luciferase reporter assays, HEK293T cells were seeded on 24-
well plates at a density of 5×104 cells/well and transfected with
150 ng of a generated construct, 0, 10, or 30 ng of pRC/CMV-HA-E2F-1,
and 1 ng of the control plasmid pCAGGS-lacZ for normalization of
transfection efﬁciency. The total amounts of plasmid DNA were
normalized to 181 ng with the pcDNA3 vector (Invitrogen). Transfec-
tions were carried out using Lipofectamine reagent (Invitrogen)
following the manufacturer's instructions. Cells were harvested 24 h
after transfection and assayed for luciferase activity and β-galactosi-
dase activity with the β-gal reporter gene assay system (Roche,
Mannheim, Germany).

2.7. Adenoviral vectors and adenoviral vector-mediated expression
To exogenously express E2F-1 and pRB, we used adenovirus Cre/
loxP-regulated expression vectors (TaKaRaBio Inc.). In these vectors
pAxCALNLw, a stuffer DNA fragment sandwiched by two loxP

and concentrated in HEK293 cells. Infection was carried out by the
addition of recombinant adenoviruses to serum-containing media
following the manufacturer's instructions. LoVo and HT1080 cells
seeded on 60 mm dishes were incubated with virus-containing media
at the indicated multiplicity of infection (MOI) at 37 °C for 1 h with
constant agitation.

3.2. Analysis of the regulatory mechanism of the human FGFR 1
transcript by E2F-1
Our previous study showed that mouse FGFR 1 promoter is
regulated by E2F-1 via the E2F-responsive sequence located at +28/
+35 (Tashiro et al., 2003). Furthermore, a human FGFR 1 promoter-
luciferase construct containing a human FGFR 1 promoter with the 5′-
end residing at position −887 and the 3′-end at position +60
(designated the −887/+60 construct) relative to the transcriptional
initiation site was strongly stimulated by co-expression with wild-
type E2F-1 (Fig. 2A and B). On the other hand, neither a DNA-binding-
defective E2F-1 mutant (E2F-1(132E)) nor a transcriptional activity-
defective E2F-1 mutant (E2F-1 (1–368)) stimulated the luciferase
activity of the −887/+60 construct (Fig. 2B). These results showed
that transcription of the human FGFR 1 promoter as well as mouse
FGFR 1 promoter is activated by E2F-1; however, by comparing the
nucleotide sequences between the mouse and human FGFR 1
promoter region, the E2F-responsive sequence located at +28/+35
in the mouse FGFR 1 promoter is not conserved in the human FGFR 1
promoter (Fig. 1A); therefore, we addressed the mechanism of E2F-1-
induced transcriptional activation of the human FGFR 1 promoter.
E2F activity is accompanied by binding to E2F-binding sequences
in target genes. The E2F-binding sequence was originally identiﬁed as
‘TTTCGCGC’ in the adenovirus E2 promoter (Kovesdi et al., 1986).
Thus, we searched for putative E2F-binding sequences in the human
FGFR 1 promoter region (−887/+60) using TFSEARCH (ver. 1.3)
with a threshold score of 85.0, and made three hits at positions
−351/−344 (A; 5′-GCGCGAAA-3′, score=100), −183/−176 (B; 5′-
ACGCCAAA-3′, score=87.4), and −114/−107 (C; 5′-TTTGGGGC-3′,
score=86.2) (Fig. 2A); however, as shown in Fig. 2B, the −95/+60
construct, which lacked all putative E2F-1-binding sequences,
remained fully responsive to wild-type E2F-1, indicating that the
−351/−344, −183/−176, and −114/−107 regions were not
critical E2F-1-responsive sites. Furthermore, the −95/+10 construct,
a 3′ deletion mutant of the −95/+60 construct, showed a reduced
response to E2F-1 (Fig. 2B). These results suggested that the E2F-1-
responsive sequence would be within the +10/+60 region of
human FGFR1 promoter.

Moreover, we examined whether other members of E2F family
could regulate human FGFR 1 transcription. As shown in Fig. 3G, E2F-2
and E2F-3a, “activator” E2Fs, stimulated the luciferase activity of the

activity of the −95/+60 constructs. As the expression levels of E2F-4
and E2F-5 were not lower than that of E2F-1, E2F-2, and E2F-3a

responses to E2F-2 and E2F-3a as well as E2F-1. These results strongly
suggested that E2F-2 and E2F-3a could also activate the transcription
of human FGFR 1 gene by binding to both +4/+22 and +25/+43
regions.

4. Discussion
Signaling through FGFRs is critical for several biological activities,
including cell growth and differentiation, angiogenesis, malignant
transformation and embryonal development. Deregulated FGFR
expression is seen in many types of human tumors, and frequently
correlates with both tumor progression and a poor prognosis;
however, the mechanism of the transcriptional regulation of human
FGFRs has not been well understood. In this study, we identiﬁed the
transcriptional initiation site of human FGFR 1 gene by 5′-RACE
(Fig. 1) and characterized the human FGFR 1 promoter to address
the mechanism by which human FGFR 1 expression is regulated by
E2F-1.
Previously, we have reported that overexpression of cyclin D1
induced FGFR 1 expression via the pRB/E2F pathway in mouse
rodent ﬁbroblasts and enhanced the mitogenic response to FGF-2
(Tashiro et al., 2003). Furthermore, more recently, it has been
reported that the inhibition of cyclin D1 using constitutively active
cyclin D1 antisense cDNA expression vector resulted in the
suppression of FGFR 1-IIIc mRNA expression, one of the alternative
splice variants of FGFR 1, in human pancreatic cancer PANC-1 cells
(Chen et al., 2008). In the present study, we also found that the
expression of FGFR 1 is up-regulated in E2F-1-overexpressing LoVo
cells, and down-regulated in pRB-overexpressing HT1080 cells. Taken
together, these observations strongly suggested that the expression
of human FGFR 1 is regulated by the cyclin D1/pRB/E2F pathway in
human cells.
Previous studies have suggested that the E2F family members play
important roles in the transcriptional regulation of various genes
through the consensus E2F binding sequence, TTTSSCGC (S=C or G).
On the other hand, in this study we demonstrated that transcription of
the human FGFR 1 gene is regulated by E2F-1 through two non-
consensus E2F-1 responsive sequences located at both +4/+22 and
+25/+43. It has also been shown that several E2F-regulated genes,
such as human cyclin A promoter (AGTCGCGG), mouse cyclin E
promoter (GCGCGA and GCGGGA), human cyclin D3 promoter
(CGCGCGCGC), and human ASK-1 promoter (GGGCGGGC), do not
contain consensus E2F binding sequences (Schulze et al.,1995; Botz et
al., 1996; Ma et al., 2003; Kherrouche et al., 2006). Recently, the
combination of chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) and high-
throughput DNA microarray technology (chip) has provided a power-
ful method (ChIP-chip) for mapping protein–DNA interactions in vivo.
This technique can identify target genes whose regulatory regions are
bound by transcription factors through both protein–DNA and
protein–protein interactions. To identify the E2F-1 target genes,
Bieda et al. (2006) examined the localization of E2F-1-binding sites
using ChIP-chip analysis. In their report, they predicted that only ∼12%
of the experimentally determined E2F-1-binding sequences possess
the consensus motif, but ∼50% of the experimentally determined E2F-
1-binding sequences possess the 1–3 b.p. mismatch compared to
consensus E2F-binding sequences. Recently, it has been proposed that
mainly four types of speculated models account for E2F-1-induced
transactivation via non-consensus E2F-1 binding sequences (Rabino-
vich et al., 2008). (1) E2F-1 indirectly binds to a non-consensus
sequence via association with another DNA-binding protein. (2) E2F-1
binds to a consensus sequence located at a distance from the
transcriptional initiation site and cross-links to the proximal promoter
region because of a looping mechanism. (3) E2F-1 is recruited to the
DNA via protein–protein interactions, and interaction of the complex
with the promoter region is stabilized by E2F-1 binding to a non-
consensus sequence. (4) E2F-1 directly binds to a non-consensus
sequence. In the case of human FGFR 1 promoter, apart from two non-
consensus sequences that are responsible for E2F-1-mediated tran-
scriptional regulation, we found another three consensus E2F-1
binding sequences located in position −351/−344 (GCGCGAA),
−183/−176 (ACGCCAAA) and −114/−107 (TTTGGGGC) in the
promoter. Among them, the distal consensus sequence (−351/
−344) could directly bind to E2F-1 in vitro evaluated by EMSA (data
not shown); however, luciferase activity of the −95/+60 construct of
human FGFR 1 promoter, which eliminates the distal consensus
sequence (−351/−344), could be activated by E2F-1 at the same level
as that of −887/+60 construct (Fig. 2B), suggesting that a looping
E2F/DNA interaction (model (2)) is not involved in the regulation
mechanism of human FGFR 1 promoter. We also demonstrated that
both +4/+22 and +25/+43 sequences might directly bind to E2F-1
in vitro, as judged from EMSA (Fig. 3B), strongly suggesting that direct
interaction (model (3 or 4)) but not indirect interaction (model (1)) of
E2F-1 with non-consensus sequence is responsible for the transcrip-
tional regulation of human FGFR 1 promoter.
If model (3) were the case, another factor would be involved in this
model. So far, there have been several reports that E2F family
members and another factor cooperatively activate target genes; for
example, it has been reported that the interaction between YY1 and
E2F-2 is believed to allow stable recruitment of E2F-2 to the cdc6
promoter (Schlisio et al., 2002) whereas the interaction of E2F-3 with
TFE3 is crucial in determining E2F-3-speciﬁc target genes (Giangrande
et al., 2003). E2F-1 was reported to activate the POLA1 promoter via
interaction with CUX1 (Truscott et al., 2008). The human FGFR 1
promoter lacks CUX1- and YY1-binding sequence motifs, but contains
several GC boxes and Sp1-binding sequence motifs. It has been
described that E2F-1 and Sp1 synergistically act in activating the DHFR
promoter (Lin et al.,1996). The transcription of ARF promoter was also
reported to be cooperatively regulated by E2F and Sp1-like factor
(Parisi et al., 2002). Furthermore, it has been shown that Sp1 is an
activator of chicken FGFR 1 gene transcription (Parakati and DiMario,
2002). We also observed that the responsiveness of human FGFR 1
promoter to Sp1 binding-defective E2F-1 mutant (E2F-1 del (113–
120) (Lin et al.,1996)) was weaker than that to wild-type E2F-1. These
observations suggest that transcription of the human FGFR 1 gene is
cooperatively activated by E2F-1 and Sp1. Further studies would be
required to elucidate the involvement of Sp1 in E2F-1-induced FGFR 1
promoter transactivation.

of
human
