# Regulation of the human Sox9 promoter by the CCAAT-binding factor

David C. Colter, Sonsoles Piera-Velazquez, David F. Hawkins, Mary Kate Whitecavage, Sergio A. Jimenez, David G. Stokes

Department of Medicine, Division of Rheumatology, Thomas Jefferson University, Jefferson Medical College,

233 South 10th Street, Room 511 BLSSB, Philadelphia, PA 19107, USA

###### Abstract

Sox9 is an essential transcriptional regulator of chondrogenesis and chondrocyte-specific gene expression; however, the identity and function of transcription factors that regulate Sox9 gene expression are not well understood. Here, we have undertaken an analysis of the human Sox9 proximal promoter region in an effort to elucidate the function and identity of transcriptional regulators that are important for controlling Sox9 gene transcription. By transfection analysis, we show that elements residing between \(-256\) bp and \(+67\) bp are important for the overall level of Sox9 promoter activity. Previously, two CCAAT boxes were identified in the Sox9 mouse and human promoters (position \(-60\) bp and \(-100\) bp) by sequence analysis (Kanai, Y., Koopman, P., 1999. Structural and functional characterization of the mouse Sox9 promoter: implications for campoemic dysplasia. Hum. Mol. Genet., 8: 691\(-\)696). We demonstrate by electrophoretic mobility shift (EMSA) competition and supershift assays that the CCAAT-binding factor (CBF) can form a complex with both Sox9 CCAAT boxes in nuclear extracts from multiple cell lines. Transfection of human Sox9 promoter-luciferase constructs containing mutated or deleted CCAAT boxes demonstrated that both CCAAT boxes are important for Sox9 promoter activity in chondrogenic cell lines and primary chondrocytes. Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) experiments demonstrated that CBF interacts with the Sox9 promoter in vivo. Together, these studies show that the Sox9 promoter is regulated by CBF through its interaction with two functional CCAAT boxes.

## 1 Introduction

Sox9 is an HMG DNA-binding domain transcription factor that is expressed during skeletal development in mesenchymal cells, pre-chondrocytes, and differentiated chondrocytes but not in hypertrophic chondrocytes (Wright et al., 1995; Healy et al., 1996; Ng et al., 1997; Mertin et al., 1999). Sox9, in cooperation with other transcription factors (such as L-Sox5 and Sox6), is a potent transcriptional activator of the COL2A1 gene through the intronic enhancer (Bell et al., 1997; Lefebvre et al., 1997, 1998; Ng et al., 1997). Sox9 also controls the expression of other chondrocyte-specific extracellular matrix protein genes such as aggrecan, COL11A2 and, as shown by our work, COL9A1 (Bridgewater et al., 1998; Sekiya et al., 2000; Zhang et al., 2003). Pivotal work in mice has shown that Sox9 is essential for chondrogenesis to proceed and is indeed required for multiple steps along the chondrocyte differentiation pathway. In mice that are chimeric for a knock-out of the Sox9 gene, cells that are Sox9\({}^{-/-}\) do not take part in mesenchymal condensation and hence chondrogenesis, and they do not express extracellular matrix genes known to be activated by Sox9 in chondrocytes (Bi et al., 1999). Further work in which the Sox9 gene was conditionally knocked-out in undifferentiated limb bud mesenchymal cells or in mesenchymal cells after undergoing condensation has revealed that Sox9 is vital for several different steps of chondrogenic differentiation to occur (Akiyama et al.,

2002). Sox9 was found to be required for mesenchymal cells to initiate condensation, and for chondrogenesis to proceed following condensation; and was also implicated in regulating the conversion of prehypertrophic into hypertrophic chondrocytes during endochondral bone formation in the growth plate (Akiyama et al., 2002). Based on the substantial evidence linking Sox9 to chondrocyte function and differentiation, Sox9 is considered to be a master regulator of chondrocyte-specific gene expression and chondrogenesis (Bell et al., 1997; Lefebvre et al., 1997; Ng et al., 1997; Bridgewater et al., 1998; Bi et al., 1999; Sekiya et al., 2000; Akiyama et al., 2002; Zhang et al., 2003). In support of this, mutations in and translocations near the Sox9 gene cause the genetic disease campomelic dysplasia (CD) that is characterized by dwarfism, hypoxia of endochondrally formed skeletal elements along with non-skeletal defects such as sex-reversal and kidney, heart and central nervous system anomalies (Foster et al., 1994; Wagner et al., 1994; Kwok et al., 1995; Schafer et al., 1995, 1996; Foster, 1996).

Although there is substantial information about the tissue and temporal-specific expression profiles of Sox9 and also which genes are targets of Sox9, information on the types of factors that are known to regulate the level of Sox9 gene transcription is scarce. The handful of potential factors for which the most information exists include the fibroblast growth factors, the cytokines IL-1b and TNF-a and certain members of the hedgehog family of proteins. In contrast, IL-1b and TNF-a cause a dramatic suppression of Sox9 gene expression in primary chondrocytes and MC615 cells (immortalized mouse chondrocytes) (Murakami et al., 2000b). The suppression of Sox9 gene transcription appears to be mediated through the direct/indirect action of NF-kB (Murakami et al., 2000b). In certain instances, BMP-2 and 4 have been shown to up-regulate Sox9 expression while retinoic acid yielded mixed results (Zehentner et al., 1999; Murakami et al., 2000a; Sekiya et al., 2000, 2001; Semba et al., 2000; Uusitalo et al., 2001). Recent findings have shown that hedgehog protein family members can up-regulate Sox9 expression, apparently through the promoter region (Tavella et al., 2004).

There have been only a few studies reported concerning the functional analysis of the Sox9 promoter. In one, Kanai and Koopman (1999) compared the sequence of the mouse and human Sox9 proximal promoter regions and performed some functional analyses of the mouse promoter. The sequence analysis revealed conserved transcription factor binding sites for GATA, CREB and two sites for CBF/NF-Y, along with a possible SOX/Sry binding site. A DNaseI hypersensitive site within the mouse Sox9 proximal promoter region (approximately between position \(-200\) to \(-70\) bp) was observed in gonadal somatic cells, undifferentiated C3H10T1/2 cells and L-3T3 cells, but not in liver cells. The mouse Sox9 proximal promoter activity was also assessed in C3H10T1/2 and L-3T3 cells, and in primary testes, ovary and liver cells. Promoter activity was high in C3H10T1/2 and L-3T3 cells until a deletion past \(-193\) bp relative to the transcriptional start site was made, similar to our results with the human Sox9 promoter in which activity decreased markedly when a deletion was made past position \(-172\) bp (Fig. 1). However, cells of the chondrocyte lineage or cells undergoing chondrogenesis in vitro were not investigated in this work.

More recently, Morishitu et al. (Morishita et al., 2001) reported on the activity of the human Sox9 promoter region in several different cell types, including rat articular chondrocytes (RACs), rat chondrosarcoma cells (RCS), ATDC5 cells, C3H10T1/2 cells and MC3T3-E1 cells. Their work showed that promoter activity decreased as much as 6-fold when deletions were made past \(-1453\) bp up to \(-113\) bp in RACs and approximately \(3-5\)-fold in RCS, ATDC5 and MC3T3-E1 cells. The highest activity was observed with a promoter fragment starting at position \(-734\) bp relative to the transcriptional start site. Interestingly, in this work, an enhancer element within the first intron was reported to function mainly in undifferentiated ATDC5 cells, where it resulted in approximately an 8-fold increase in promoter activity. The enhancer element was less active in RACs and RCS cells, in which it resulted in a 2-fold increase. The significance of this putative enhancer region is not clear since the enhancer was efficient only in undifferentiated ATDC5 cells. Nuclear factor binding to the enhancer element was demonstrated by electromobility shift assays (EMSA) and, combined with the promoter-reporter experiments, showed that the enhancer consisted of a 30 bp sequence that is conserved between mouse and human. Despite the extensive studies reviewed above, transcription factors that interact with the Sox9 proximal promoter region and/or the upstream elements have yet to be identified and functionally characterized. Here, we describe our studies on the identification and functional analysis of transcription factors that interact with the Sox9 proximal promoter. We demonstrate in this work that the two CCAAT boxes found within the human Sox9 proximal promoter region can specifically bind to the CCAAT-binding factor and that mutation or deletion of the CCAAT boxes diminishes Sox9 promoter activity.

## Results

### Deletion analysis of the human Sox9 promoter

In order to determine which regions of the human Sox9 proximal promoter are important for transcriptional activity, we cloned a 1.1 kb fragment encompassing the region from \(-1034\) to \(+67\) bp relative to the transcriptional start site (\(+1\)) into the pGL3-luciferase reporter vector. Sequence analysis of this region predicts a number of relevant transcription factor binding sites, including one Sry site, two @EF1 sites, one NFAT site, adjacent NF-kB, cREL and Sp1 sites, a one-half CREB site and two CCAAT sites (Fig. 1A top) (Kanai and Koopman, 1999). A series of 5'-deletion constructs (Fig. 1A bottom) were prepared and the constructs were tested for promoter activity in the mouse mesenchymal cell line C3H10T1/2, the embryonal carcinoma ATDC5 cell line and in fetal bovine epiphyseal chondrocytes. In C3H10T1/2 cells, we observed that the activity of the Sox9 promoter decreased by \(\sim\)35% when a deletion was introduced up to position \(-839\) bp and that the activity remained at this level through a deletion to position \(-534\) bp (Fig. 1B). When a deletion to position \(-256\) bp was introduced, promoter activity rose to levels observed for the full-length \(-1034\)/\(+67\) bp construct (Fig. 1B). A further deletion to position \(-218\) bp reduced Sox9 promoter activity to approximately \(60-80\)% of the \(-1034\)/\(+67\) bp construct (Fig. 1B). A dramatic reduction in promoter activity (\(33\)% of the \(-1034\)/\(+67\) construct), however, was observed when a deletion to \(-122\) was introduced (Fig. 1B). When a deletion construct to position \(-172\) bp was tested, the Sox9 promoter activity was similarly reduced to approximately \(25-30\)% of the full length \(-1034\)/\(+67\) bp construct (Fig. 1C). Fig. 1D shows that the deletion constructs yielded similar results when tested in the chondrocyte ATDC5 cell line. Lastly, when the full length \(-1034\)/\(+67\) bp construct, the \(-534\) bp construct and the \(-172\) bp and \(-122\) bp constructs were transfected into fetal bovine epiphyseal chondrocytes, the relative

activities (as compared to the \(-\)1034/+67 bp construct) were also similar to what was observed in the chondrogenic cell lines (Fig. 1E). From these experiments, we conclude that important human Sox9 proximal promoter elements reside within the region extending from \(-\)256 bp to the transcriptional start site, confirming previous results reported for the mouse and human Sox9 promoters (Kanai and Koopman, 1999; Morishita et al., 2001).

### CBF binds to the CCAAT-1 box in the human Sox9 proximal promoter

Based on a sequence comparison of the Sox9 proximal promoter regions from mouse, chick and human, Kanai and Koopman (1999) predicted binding sites for CREB and CBF, among others, within the region from \(-\)172 bp to \(+\)1 bp (Fig. 2). In order to determine which cis-elements within the Sox9 proximal promoter region are important for transcriptional activity, we investigated whether the CCAAT-1 box (Fig. 2) within the Sox9 proximal promoter specifically interacted with CBF/NF-Y in nuclear extracts from chondrocyte cell lines. Shown in Fig. 3 are the results of a competition and supershift EMSA for CBF/NF-Y binding. Fig. 3A shows that specific complexes form with a probe containing the wild type (WT) Sox9 CCAAT-1 box (Fig. 3D) in nuclear extracts from ATDC5 cells, C3H10T1/2 cells, HTB human chondrosarcoma cells, NIH3T3 cells and RCS rat chondrosarcoma cells. Interestingly, two closely spaced DNA\(-\)protein complexes were observed in ATDC5, C3H10T1/2 and NIH3T3 cells but not in HTB or RCS cells (Fig. 3A). In Fig. 3B, the results of a competition analysis with WT Sox9 and consensus and mutated CCAAT box (sequences shown in Fig. 3D), and ATDC5 cell nuclear extract are presented. In this experiment, both complexes that formed with the WT Sox9 CCAAT-1 box probe were efficiently competed away with 100X unlabeled WT Sox9 CCAAT-1 box probe (lane 5, Fig. 3B). However, only the stronger, upper complex was competed with the unlabeled consensus CCAAT box probe (lane 6, Fig. 3B). Neither complex was competed with the unlabeled mutated consensus CCAAT box probe (lane 7, Fig. 3B). Lane 8 in Fig. 3B shows that the labeled consensus CCAAT box probe exhibited a DNA\(-\)protein complex identical to the upper complex formed with the WT Sox9 CCAAT-1 box probe. Finally, lane 10 in Fig. 3B shows that 100X unlabeled WT Sox9 CCAAT-1 box probe efficiently competed with the complex formed on the labeled consensus CCAAT box probe. In order to further determine the identity of the factor(s) binding to the Sox9 CCAAT-1 box, a supershift analysis was employed. Shown in Fig. 3C is a supershift analysis of the WT Sox9 CCAAT-1 box with anti-CBF-B antibody. As can be seen, the anti-CBF-B antibody inhibited CBF binding to both the WT Sox9 CCAAT-1 probe and to the CCAAT consensus probe in nuclear extracts from ATDC5, C3H10T1/2 and RCS cells.

We next investigated whether CBF could interact with the Sox9 CCAAT-1 and CCAAT-2 boxes if they were present on the same DNA probe. We used PCR to generate a 95 bp probe that encompassed the region containing both Sox9 CCAAT boxes. We show in Fig. 5A that when the larger probe containing both Sox9 CCAAT boxes is used in an EMSA with ATDCS cell nuclear extracts, two complexes are formed. The lower complex has the same mobility as that of a single Sox9 CCAAT box bound by CBF, whereas the other, more slowly moving complex, is presumably caused by CBF binding to both sites at once. We verified this assumption by performing a cross competition analysis shown in Fig. 5A and B. In Fig. 5A, competition with probes containing either a single WT Sox9 CCAAT-1 box or a single WT Sox9 CCAAT-2 box effectively competed binding to the double Sox9 CCAAT box probe, as did the double Sox9 CCAAT box probe itself. In Fig. 5B, we show that WT single Sox9 CCAAT box probes can compete with this binding as can a consensus CCAAT box probe. We also show that neither mutated Sox9 CCAAT-1 or CCAAT-2 box probes, nor a mutated consensus CCAAT box probe can compete with the Sox9 WT double CCAAT box probe. Lastly, we show in Fig. 5B that inclusion of an anti-CBF-B antibody inhibits binding of CBF to the Sox9 double CCAAT box probe as it did to the single Sox9 CCAAT box probes (Figs. 3C and 4B).

### The Sox9 CCAAT boxes contribute to promoter activity

In order to determine whether the CCAAT boxes within the Sox9 promoter contribute to promoter activity, a deletion of the CCAAT-1 sequence and a mutation of the CCAAT-2 box were introduced into the full length -1034/+67 bp Sox9 promoter-luciferase construct (Fig. 6D). Fig. 6A shows that when the Sox9 CCAAT-1 box is deleted (CCAAT-1d) and the CCAAT-2 box is mutated (CCAAT-2m), Sox9 promoter activity decreases to between 30 and 50% of the activity of the WT Sox9 promoter in C3H10T1/2 cells. Fig. 6B shows that deletion of the CCAAT-1 box reduces Sox9 promoter activity by approximately 50% in ATDC5 cells. In Fig. 6C, we demonstrate that deletion of the CCAAT-1 box and mutation of the CCAAT-2 box reduce Sox9 promoter activity by 50 - 60% in fetal bovine chondrocytes. Finally, in Fig. 6D, we show that a construct containing both the deleted CCAAT-1 box and the mutated CCAAT-2 box exhibits a lower transcriptional activity than the constructs containing either the deleted CCAAT-1 box or the mutated CCAAT-2 box alone in C3H10T1/2 cells.

### Association of CBF with the Sox9 promoter in vivo

In order to demonstrate that CBF interacts with the Sox9 promoter in vivo, a chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assay was carried out. In this procedure, DNA-binding proteins are covalently linked to the genomic DNA by exposure to a cross-linking agent and then the DNA-protein complexes are isolated by immunoprecipitation. After reversal of cross-links, the DNA is subjected to PCR with primers that encompass the transcription factor binding site of interest. C3H10T1/2 cells were subjected to cross-linking with formaldehyde, the cells were lysed and the chromatin was sheared by sonication. The chromatin was then immunoprecipitated with anti-CBF-B antibodies and the resulting DNA was subjected to PCR amplification with primers flanking the region containing the two Sox9 CCAAT boxes (Fig. 7A). Fig. 7B shows that anti-CBF-B antibodies specifically enriched for the region containing the Sox9 proximal promoter, as did an anti-TFIIB antibody but not a non-specific IgG antibody (control antibody, Fig. 7B).

The CCAAT box, bound most commonly by the heterotrimeric CCAAT-box binding factor, also known as Nuclear Factor-Y, is one of several common elements found in the promoters of eukaryotic genes (25% of eukaryotic promoters) (Maity and de Crombrugghe, 1998; Mantovani, 1999). CCAAT boxes are usually found between \(-60\) and \(-100\) bp of the transcriptional start site, can be in the sense or anti-sense (inverted) orientation, and multiple functional CCAAT boxes within a promoter region are not uncommon. CCAAT boxes operate at multiple levels in the transcriptional control of genes. Interaction of CBF with the CCAAT box can promote recruitment of the basal transcriptional machinery through interactions with subunits of TFIID (Bellorini et al., 1997; Frontini et al., 2002). Mutation of the CCAAT box can cause a decrease in the overall level of transcription whereas in other cases, CBF-CCAAT box interaction, along with other transcription factors, is necessary for induction of transcription by agents such as serum and steroid hormones (Kennedy et al., 1990; Franson and Bornstein, 1993; Subramanian et al., 1998; Scholz et al., 1999; Sorensen and Wintersberger, 1999; Jump et al., 2001). CBF binding to the CCAAT box has also been shown to reorganize chromatin structure around the promoters of certain genes. Interaction of CBF with DNA results in significant DNA-bending, CBF can interact with CCAAT boxes that are bound by nucleosomes and binding of CBF to the CCAAT box can result in the prevention of nucleosome interactions at the HSP70 and topoisomerase IIa promoters (Landsberger and Wolffe, 1995; Ronchi et al., 1995; Li et al., 1998; Motta et al., 1999; Coustry et al., 2001).

CBF plays an important role in the regulation of cell proliferation by interacting with the promoters of several cell-cycle related genes. Indeed, CBF, usually through interactions with multiple CCAAT boxes, regulates several genes involved in cell proliferation and control of the cell cycle, such as topoisomerase IIa, thymidine kinase, E2F1 and cdc25c (Zwicker et al., 1995; Good and Chen, 1996; Isaacs et al., 1996; Kim et al., 1997; Chang et al., 1999; Wang et al., 1999; Adachi et al., 2000; Hu and Maity, 2000; Manni et al., 2001; Magan et al., 2003). Over-expression of a dominant-negative CBF in fibroblasts inhibits cell growth and a reduction in the level of the CBF-B subunit has been observed upon serum deprivation in several cell lines (Farina et al., 1999; Hu and Maity, 2000). Interestingly, it has been reported that as myoblasts differentiate into skeletal muscle cells, CCAAT binding activity is lost as a result of a dramatic down-regulation of the levels of the CBF-B subunit (Gurtner et al., 2003). Indeed, the CBF-B subunit is not detected in adult skeletal and cardiac muscle cells. Over-expression of CBF-B in differentiating myoblast cells inhibits their withdrawal from the cell cycle. Other tissues, such as adult kidney, intestine and liver still exhibited CBF-B expression. The reduction in CBF-B in muscle appears to be post-translationally mediated.

The two Sox9 CCAAT boxes, one found at \(-60\) bp, the other at \(-100\) bp, may play multiple roles in regulating Sox9 promoter activity. The placement of the CCAAT boxes within the Sox9 promoter suggests that CBF might be involved in all of the aforementioned transcriptional regulatory roles with regard to Sox9 gene expression (cooperative interactions, interaction with basal transcriptional machinery, modification of chromatin structure). For instance, the proximal CCAAT-2 box (\(-60\) bp) may be involved in interactions with the general transcriptional machinery while the distal CCAAT-1 box (\(-100\) bp) could be responsible, at least in part, for setting up the open chromatin configuration found in this region of the Sox9 promoter and/or be involved in interactions with other upstream regulatory factors. However, it is certainly possible that both CCAAT boxes participate in all of the regulatory roles discussed above. In the case of the human Sox9 gene, the two CCAAT boxes are located 41 bp apart on center as shown in Fig. 2, while in the mouse, they are 46 bp on center, equating to approximately 4 turns of the helix. Since the CCAAT boxes are inverted relative to each other, the Sox9 CCAAT boxes are most likely on opposite faces of the DNA helix. The Sox9 CCAAT box placement, therefore, has the potential to impact on the structure of the promoter due the intrinsic DNA-bending induced by CBF/NF-Y binding and also the ability of CBF/NF-Y to alter or prevent nucleosomal interactions with DNA.

Interestingly, far upstream elements are also implicated in the control of Sox9 gene expression. As mentioned above, heterozygous mutations in the Sox9 gene resulting in haploinsufficiency are the major cause of CD (Foster et al., 1994; Wagner et al., 1994). Since no other genes have been identified in this region, the hypothesis was put forth that the rearrangements somehow disrupted control elements located far upstream that are important for Sox9 gene expression (Wunderle et al., 1998; Pfeifer et al., 1999; Bagheri-Fam et al., 2001). Further evidence for this hypothesis came from studies performed by Wunderle et al. (1998) in which human YAC clones containing either 75 or 350 kb of sequence upstream of the Sox9 gene were used to generate transgenic mice. Human Sox9 gene expression in the transgenic mice, as measured by an in frame insertion of the LacZ gene, was largely dependent on sequences located from \(-\)75 to \(-\)350 kb upstream of the Sox9 gene. However, when transgenic mice that contained multiple copies of a human YAC containing 75 kb of upstream sequence were studied, expression of Sox9 was detected, although abnormal. Specifically, loss of Sox9 expression was observed in the mandibular process and 2nd branchial arch, developing ribs and cervical vertebrae, and in the developing limbs Sox9 expression was lost in the developing long bones. These studies indicated that elements controlling the expression of Sox9 in the neuroectoderm and the developing ear may lie between 45 and 150 kb upstream whereas elements controlling expression in the limb and vertebrae are predicted to lie approximately 200\(-\)350 kb upstream (Adachi et al., 2000). In a recent sequence comparison of the upstream region of the Sox9 gene in mouse, human and the Japanese puffer fish (Fugu rubripes), five small regions of conserved sequence, denoted E1\(-\)E5, were found to reside between 28 and 290 kb upstream of both the mouse and human genes (Bagheri-Fam et al., 2001). Elements E1 and E2 lie in the regions that may control expression in the neuroectoderm and developing ear, whereas elements E3\(-\)E5 lie in the region implicated in controlling limb and vertebral column expression (Bagheri-Fam et al., 2001).

Regulation of the temporal and tissue-specific expression of the Sox9 gene is complex. CBF is known to be important for the regulation of cell cycle genes, muscle and globin gene expression during differentiation and may be involved in cellular senescence (Zwicker et al., 1995; Good and Chen, 1996; Chen, 1997; Liberati et al., 1998; Matuoka and Yu Chen, 1999; Duan et al., 2001; Manni et al., 2001; Gurtner et al., 2003; Fang et al., 2004). CBF could, therefore, be involved in chondrogenesis and chondrocyte maturation at multiple steps. ## Materials and methods

### Cell culture

C3H10T1/2 cells, NIH3T3 cells and HTB94 (HTB) human chondrosarcoma cells were obtained from ATCC. RCS cells were a kind gift from Dr. Benoit de Crombrugghe (Mukhopadhyay et al., 1995). ATDC5 cells were obtained from the RIKEN BioResource Center Cell Bank (Ibaraki, Japan). C3H10T1/2 and RCS cells were maintained in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM) containing 10% heat inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS), supplemented with L-glutamine (2 mM), penicillin (100 units/ml) and streptomycin (100 mg/ml). NIH3T3 cells were maintained in DMEM containing 10% bovine calf serum and supplements as above. HTB cells were maintained in DMEM containing 10% FBS and supplements as above. ATDC5 cells were propagated in DMEM/Ham's F-12 (1:1) containing 5% FBS, 10 mg/ml human transferrin, 3 x 10-8 M sodium selenite and supplements as above.

Bovine fetal epiphyseal cartilage from the knee was obtained from Animal Technologies, Inc. (Tyler, TX). Chondrocytes were isolated as previously described (Coimbra et al., 2004). After isolation, fetal bovine chondrocytes (FBCs) were frozen in 90% FBS/10% DMSO until future use. Upon thawing, FBCs were maintained in DMEM containing 10% FBS, supplemented with L-glutamine (2 mM), penicillin (100 units/ml), streptomycin (100 mg/ml), fungizone (2.5 mg/ml) and ascorbic acid (50 mg/ml). FBCs were cultured at a density of 5 x 106 cells in 60 mm plastic petri dishes that had been previously coated with 0.9 ml of a 10% solution of poly-(2-hydroxyethyl) methacrylate (poly-HEMA, Poly Sciences Inc., Malvern, PA) to prevent attachment as described previously (Reginato et al., 1994).

### Sox9-luciferase constructs

The human Sox9 proximal promoter region from -1034 bp to +67 bp relative to the transcriptional start site was obtained by PCR using human genomic DNA (a kind gift from Dr. Charlene Williams) as a template and the following primers: forward; 5'-GTGGAGCGTTTTGTCTGG-3', reverse; 5'-TGAAACTGGCCGAGTCTC-3'. The PCR product was cloned into the pCR-Blunt vector (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA) and then transferred as a _Kpn_1-_Eco_RV fragment into the pGL3-Basic vector (Promega, Madison, WI) that had been digested with _Kpn_1-_Sma_I. The various 5'-Sox9 promoter-deletion constructs were made by digesting the parent Sox9 promoter-luciferase construct (-1034/+67, Fig 1A) with _Kpn_1 (generates an _Exo_III protected 3'-overhang) and _Spe_I (generates an _Exo_III digestable 5'-overhang) followed by Exonuclease III treatment (Erase-a-base kit, Promega). The Sox9 promoter-luciferase CCAAT-1 deletion construct was made with the ExSite PCR-based site-directed mutagenesis kit from Stratagene (La Jolla, CA) and the following primers: CCAAT-1 box deletion; forward, 5'-CCCCAAAATCGGGTCAGCTGCC-3', and reverse, GGCAGGCAGCTGACCCGATTTGGGG. The CCAAT-2 box mutation was introduced by a two-step PCR-based mutagenesis procedure (Genuario et al., 1993) with the following primers: CCAAT-2 box mutation; forward, 5'-TGTGTTCGGCGGTGCTCTA-3' and reverse, 5'-TAGAGCCACCCGCCAACACA-3'. The double CCAAT-1+2 mutation/deletion construct was made by transferring a _Pvu_II-_Pst_I fragment containing the CCAAT-2 mutation into the CCAAT-1 deleted Sox9 promoter construct. All constructs were verified by sequencing.

### Nuclear extracts and electrophoretic mobility shift assays

Nuclear extracts were prepared from sub-confluent cells according to the method of Dignam et al. (1983) using the CellLytic NuCLEAR extraction kit (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO). Briefly, cells were placed in hypotonic buffer (10 mM HEPES [pH 7.9], 1.5 mM MgCl2, 10 mM KCl and 0.5 mM DTT) and incubated on ice for 15 min. Igeapal CA-630 was added to a final concentration of 0.6% and the mixture was vortexed vigorously for 10 s. Nuclei were recovered by centrifugation at 3300 x _g_ for 30 s at 4 degC and extracted in buffer containing 20 mM HEPES pH 7.9, 0.42 M NaCl, 25% glycerol, 1.5 mM MgCl2, 0.2 mM EDTA and 0.5 mM DTT for 30 min at 4 degC by gentle shaking. The extract was then centrifuged for 15 min at 25,000 x _g_, and the supernatant was snap frozen at -70 degC. All buffers contained a protease inhibitor cocktail (2 mM 4-(2-amino-ethyl) benzenesulfonylfluoride, 1.4 pM _trans_-epoxysuccinyl-L-leucylamido [4-guanidinobutane], 130 pM bestatin, 1 mM leupeptin and 0.3 pM aprotinin; Sigma-Aldrich).

Electromobility shift assays (EMSAs) were carried out as previously described with minor modifications (Stokes and Perry, 1995; Stokes et al., 2001; Zhang et al., 2003). Briefly, binding reactions consisted of 12.5 mM Hepes, pH 7.9, 50-100 mM NaCl, 5% glycerol, 2 mg/ml BSA, 1-2 mg poly-dldC, 0.1 mM EDTA, 0.1 mM DTT, 0.5-1 ng of 32P-end labeled double stranded oligonucleotide probes (20-35 bp) or 4-5ng end labeled PCR-generated probe (95 bp) and 10-15 mg of nuclear protein. Binding reactions were incubated for 30 min at RT and then loaded onto either 5% acrylamide-0.5X tris-borate-EDTA gels (95 bp CCAAT-1+2 probe) or 50 mM Tris/0.38 M glycine-2 mM EDTA gels (double stranded oligonucleotide single CCAAT-box probes) and electrophoresed at 130 V for 2-3 h. For competition analyses, 100-fold excess of unlabeled-competitor probe was included in the binding reaction. For the supershift experiments, 1-2 ml of anti-CBF-B antibody (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Santa Cruz, CA) was added to the binding reaction for 25 min at room temperature (RT)

prior to the addition of labeled DNA probe. After addition of labeled DNA-probe, the binding reaction was incubated for an additional 20 min at RT.

### Transient transfection and luciferase assays

All cell types were transfected with the FuGENE 6 transfection reagent (Roche, Indianapolis, IN). C3H10T1/2 cells were plated at 50,000 cells/well and ATDC5 cells at 100,000 cells/well in 6-well plates 24 h before transfection. Cell lines were transfected with 2 mg DNA/well (1.5 mg construct and 0.5 mg pCMVb as a control for transfection efficiency). FBCs were transfected utilizing the procedure of Madry and Trippel et al. (Madry and Trippel, 2000), with modifications. FBCs were maintained in polyHEMA coated dishes in suspension culture in order to preserve the differentiated phenotype (Reginato et al., 1994; Stokes et al., 2002). FBCs in suspension culture were digested overnight with 0.5 mg/ml collagenase in DMEM containing 10% FBS and supplements. After digestion, the cells were plated into 24-well plates at 75,000/well and grown to ~70% confluence. The FBCs were then treated with 10 U/ml hyaluronidase for 4 h before and during transfection. FBCs were transfected with 1 mg DNA/well (0.5 mg construct and 0.5 mg pCMVb). Cells were harvested 36-42 h after transfection and luciferase assays were performed with a luciferase assay kit (Promega) and a Turner Designs TD 20/20 luminometer (Turner Designs, Sunnyvale CA). b-galactosidase assays were performed spectrophotometrically with a b-galactosidase enzyme assay system (Promega). Protein concentrations in the cell lysates were determined using the commassie-blue protein assay (Pierce, Rockford, IL). Luciferase activity was normalized to both b-galactosidase activity and protein concentration. Transfection data represent at least three independent experiments each performed in triplicate.

### Chromatin immunoprecipitation assay

The chromatin immunoprecipitation assay was performed using the Chip-IT kit (Active Motif, Carlsbad, CA) as per manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, three 10 cm2 plates of 70-80%-confluent C3H10T1/2 cells were treated with 1% formaldehyde in PBS for 10 min at RT. The formaldehyde was inactivated by the addition of 0.125 M glycine in PBS to the cells for 5 min at RT. The cells were then washed in ice-cold PBS and harvested by scraping. The cells were then resuspended in lysis buffer and the nuclei released by Dounce homogenization. The nuclei were pelleted by centrifugation at 2400 \(\times g\) for 10 min at 4 degC. To shear genomic DNA, the nuclei were then sonicated on ice with fifteen 20 s pulses using a Vibra Cell sonicator (Sonics and Materials, Inc., Danbury, CT) set at 30% output. The sheared DNA was then centrifuged at 12,000 \(\times g\) for 12 min at 4 degC and the supernatant collected. For each immunoprecipitation, 50 ml of chromatin was pre-cleared with Protein G agarose beads for 1-2 h at 4 degC. One to three mg of the appropriate antibody was incubated with a pre-cleared chromatin aliquot overnight at 4 degC with gentle rotation. The antibody-chromatin mixtures were then incubated with Protein G beads for 1.5 h at 4 degC. The antibody/chromatin/Protein G bead mixtures were then washed extensively and the DNA eluted. Crosslinks were reversed and the DNA was purified for PCR analysis. Primers flanking the two CCAAT boxes in the mouse Sox9 promoter were employed (see Fig. 7A). The sequences of the primers utilized in Fig. 7C that amplify a 153 bp segment approximately 3 kb upstream of the Sox9 transcriptional start site were forward: 5'-CAGAACTGACCGCAAAGCAACACG-3' and reverse: 5'-CACTGCAATTTTCAGCAACATCC-3'.
